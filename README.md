## How to use this roadmap

1. Follow the stages in order. Learn the market mechanics and risk concepts before researching strategies.
2. Use the subject directories to explore specific topics once prerequisites are understood.
3. Prefer regulator, exchange, broker, protocol and project documentation over anonymous signals or promotional claims.
4. Start with historical data and paper trading. Do not connect a new bot to a funded account.
5. Track the source, license, timestamp, assumptions, transaction costs and limitations of every dataset or result.

### Resource labels

- **Open source:** code can be inspected under a stated license; verify license and maintenance status.
- **Free / freemium / paid:** product pricing may change and free tiers may have limits.
- **Custodial:** another provider controls or safeguards assets under its service terms.
- **Self-custody:** the user controls private keys or recovery material and is responsible for backups and signing.
- **Exchange / broker / protocol:** these are different service types. A decentralized exchange interface is not a regulated stockbroker; a wallet is not an exchange account.
- **Verification status:** links are curated from known official domains and selected official documentation. This README does not claim that every product feature, pricing tier, country access rule or current operational status has been independently tested. Confirm those details at the linked official source.

## Learning roadmap: zero to advanced

### Stage 0. Financial literacy

**Goal:** Money, inflation, compounding, savings vs investing vs trading, fees, scams, risk and return.

### Stage 1. Mathematics and statistics

**Goal:** Percentages, returns, probability, expected value, distributions, correlation, regression and uncertainty.

### Stage 2. Economics

**Goal:** GDP, inflation, rates, monetary/fiscal policy, bond yields, currency and official economic data.

### Stage 3. Market structure

**Goal:** Primary/secondary markets, orders, spreads, liquidity, clearing, settlement, custody and market impact.

### Stage 4. Indian market infrastructure

**Goal:** SEBI, NSE/BSE, MCX/NCDEX, brokers, demat, NSDL/CDSL, contract notes, taxes and investor grievance routes.

### Stage 5. Global market infrastructure

**Goal:** US/global regulators, exchanges, settlement, currency risk, cross-border restrictions and time zones.

### Stage 6. Instruments

**Goal:** Equities, bonds, funds, ETFs, REITs, commodities, FX, derivatives and cryptoassets.

### Stage 7. Accounting

**Goal:** Income statement, balance sheet, cash flow, notes, debt, working capital, earnings quality and disclosures.

### Stage 8. Equity research and valuation

**Goal:** Business models, ratios, DCF, comparable companies, scenarios, governance and written research notes.

### Stage 9. Long-term investing

**Goal:** Asset allocation, diversification, benchmarks, rebalancing, costs, factor exposure and behavioral biases.

### Stage 10. Funds and index investing

**Goal:** NAV, tracking error, ETF mechanics, fund disclosures, fees, mandate and tax considerations.

### Stage 11. IPOs

**Goal:** Offer documents, fresh issue vs OFS, valuation, dilution, ASBA/UPI mechanics and listing risks.

### Stage 12. Technical analysis

**Goal:** OHLCV, indicators, market structure, rule definition, limitations and bias-aware testing.

### Stage 13. Trading risk

**Goal:** Position sizing, risk per trade, drawdown, slippage, costs, journals, risk of ruin and psychology.

### Stage 14. Index trading

**Goal:** Index construction, futures/options, expiry, settlement, leverage and index-specific rules.

### Stage 15. Futures

**Goal:** Contract specifications, basis, margin, mark-to-market, rollover, hedging and gap risk.

### Stage 16. Options

**Goal:** Payoffs, Greeks, volatility, theta, spreads, assignment/settlement and scenario analysis.

### Stage 17. Advanced derivatives

**Goal:** Portfolio Greeks, hedging, volatility skew, stress tests, liquidity and tail risk.

### Stage 18. International investing

**Goal:** Global equities, currency conversion, tax, country risk and eligibility rules.

### Stage 19. Fixed income

**Goal:** Bond pricing, yield, duration, convexity, credit risk and yield curves.

### Stage 20. FX and commodities

**Goal:** Pairs, forwards/futures, commodity curves, carry, storage costs and leverage.

### Stage 21. Crypto and blockchain

**Goal:** Keys, wallets, exchanges, stablecoins, on-chain data, DeFi, smart contracts, security and jurisdiction.

### Stage 22. Quantitative finance

**Goal:** Time series, stationarity, factor models, portfolio optimization, hypothesis testing and overfitting.

### Stage 23. Python for finance

**Goal:** Pandas, NumPy, data cleaning, APIs, SQL, visualization, testing and reproducible environments.

### Stage 24. Algorithmic trading

**Goal:** Strategy specification, backtesting, transaction costs, out-of-sample tests, walk-forward testing and paper trading.

### Stage 25. APIs and data engineering

**Goal:** REST/WebSocket, authentication, data licenses, rate limits, idempotency, stale-data handling and logging.

### Stage 26. Strategy research

**Goal:** Value, quality, momentum, trend, mean reversion, factor, event-driven and hedging hypotheses.

### Stage 27. Institutional portfolio management

**Goal:** Mandates, gross/net exposure, prime brokerage, fund fees, factor risk, liquidity and reporting.

### Stage 28. Risk management

**Goal:** Market, credit, liquidity, model, counterparty and operational risk; VaR/CVaR and stress testing.

### Stage 29. Controlled implementation

**Goal:** Historical research → paper trading → read-only monitoring → broker sandbox → optional live use after independent checks.

### Stage 30. News and economic data

**Goal:** Filings, central-bank releases, calendars, source validation, event studies and revisions.

### Stage 31. Financial technology

**Goal:** Market-data pipelines, OMS/EMS, FIX basics, audit logs, access control and disaster recovery.

### Stage 32. Capstones

**Goal:** Company report, portfolio dashboard, option calculator, backtest, paper-trading system and risk report.


### Stage completion checklist

- [ ] I can explain the core concepts without relying on a signal provider.
- [ ] I can find and verify primary-source information.
- [ ] I can calculate relevant returns, costs and risk metrics.
- [ ] I can reproduce the analysis from documented inputs.
- [ ] I can identify data leakage, look-ahead bias, survivorship bias and overfitting.
- [ ] I can describe failure cases, liquidity constraints and operational risks.
- [ ] I can explain why historical results do not guarantee future performance.

### Storage safety checklist

- Never share seed phrases, private keys, recovery codes or signing approvals.
- Download wallet software only from the official domain and verify the publisher.
- Test recovery procedures with small, non-critical amounts before relying on a setup.
- Consider hardware wallets or multisignature arrangements when the threat model warrants them.
- Verify network, token contract, destination address and transaction details before signing.
- Use a password manager and phishing-resistant multifactor authentication where supported.
- Understand that self-custody shifts key-loss and transaction-error risk to the user; custodial services introduce provider, legal, operational and insolvency risks.
- Do not assume a wallet, custodian or exchange is insured or protected in the same way as a bank deposit.

### Understand the trading method before using a venue

- **Cash equities / spot:** buying or selling the underlying asset, subject to venue and settlement rules.
- **Long-term investing:** a portfolio approach with a defined horizon and allocation; not the same as intraday trading.
- **Intraday / swing / positional:** time horizons, not guarantees of a strategy's quality.
- **Limit / market / stop orders:** different execution and price risks; stop orders do not guarantee a fill at the trigger price.
- **Futures and options:** leveraged or nonlinear instruments with contract-specific margin, expiry and settlement risk.
- **FX and commodity derivatives:** product eligibility, leverage, settlement and local regulation matter.
- **Crypto spot:** custody, token, exchange, chain, liquidity and jurisdiction risks.
- **Decentralized finance:** smart-contract, oracle, bridge, liquidation, token and governance risks.
- **Paper trading:** useful for workflow testing, but simulated fills and market impact may be unrealistic.

## Safe automation architecture

A responsible research system separates components instead of letting an AI agent directly control a funded account without controls.

1. **Data ingestion:** authorized sources, timestamps, market calendars, licensing, stale-data checks.
2. **Strategy research:** explicit hypotheses, reproducible features, version-controlled code and independent test sets.
3. **Backtest engine:** point-in-time data, realistic fills, fees, spread, slippage, corporate actions and liquidity constraints.
4. **Risk engine:** exposure caps, position limits, order limits, loss thresholds and kill switch.
5. **Paper execution:** simulated orders, partial-fill handling, cancellations and reconciliation.
6. **Monitoring:** logs, alerts, heartbeat, latency, failed requests, stale data and service availability.
7. **Credential controls:** secret manager or environment variables; never commit keys to source control.
8. **Manual approval gate:** live trading stays disabled until legal, broker, risk and operational checks are independently completed.

### API key minimum precautions

- Use read-only keys for research whenever possible.
- For paper-trading, use sandbox credentials rather than live credentials.
- Disable withdrawals on trading API keys wherever the platform permits.
- Restrict IP access where supported and rotate compromised keys immediately.
- Do not paste credentials into chats, notebooks, logs, screenshots or GitHub.
- Use least privilege, two-factor authentication and account notifications.
- Test the kill switch and order-cancellation procedure in simulation.
- Treat AI-generated code and trading logic as untrusted until reviewed and tested.

### AI and autonomous agents

“Autonomous” can mean generating research, proposing trades, placing orders, or managing a portfolio. These are different levels of authority. Begin with offline analysis and human review. An LLM can hallucinate data, misunderstand an API, mis-handle market state or generate invalid code; it should not be treated as a reliable risk manager. Require deterministic checks, hard limits, audit logs and a manual stop mechanism outside the model.

## Paper-trading progression

### Gate 1 — Historical analysis
- Use data with documented provenance and license.
- Compare with a simple baseline.
- Include transaction costs and realistic timing.
- Test for look-ahead, survivorship and selection bias.

### Gate 2 — Out-of-sample research
- Freeze strategy rules before evaluating held-out data.
- Run walk-forward and sensitivity analysis.
- Record failed experiments and parameter changes.
- Avoid choosing a strategy solely because it had the best historical return.

### Gate 3 — Paper trading
- Simulate orders using live or delayed data as clearly labeled.
- Record intended and simulated fills.
- Measure latency, spread assumptions, rejected orders and slippage.
- Keep a trading journal and compare expected versus realized simulated risk.

### Gate 4 — Read-only monitoring
- Validate quotes and timestamps.
- Handle disconnects and stale data.
- Do not place live orders.

### Gate 5 — Broker sandbox
- Test authentication, order lifecycle, cancellations, errors, rate limits and reconciliation.
- Confirm current API permissions and market-specific rules.

### Gate 6 — Optional live use
This is not a required learning outcome. Before any live activity, independently verify eligibility, current regulation, taxes, fees, liquidity, maximum loss, margin, operational failure modes and the amount of capital that could be lost without impairing essential needs. Do not start with borrowed money or leveraged derivatives.

## Glossary

- **Asset allocation:** distribution of a portfolio among asset classes.
- **Backtest:** simulation of a strategy on historical data.
- **Basis:** relationship between spot and futures prices.
- **Bid–ask spread:** difference between the best displayed bid and ask.
- **Custody:** safeguarding or control of financial assets or keys.
- **Drawdown:** decline from a portfolio's previous peak.
- **ETF:** exchange-traded fund.
- **Exchange:** venue or market operator where eligible instruments trade.
- **Futures:** standardized derivative contracts with defined expiry and obligations.
- **Implied volatility:** volatility inferred from an option's market price under a pricing model.
- **Leverage:** exposure created relative to the capital committed.
- **Liquidity:** ability to transact without excessive delay or price impact.
- **Market order:** order prioritizing execution rather than a guaranteed price.
- **Option:** contract giving a defined right under specified terms; payoff depends on contract type.
- **Paper trading:** simulated trading without real-money order execution.
- **Self-custody:** control of private keys or signing authority by the user.
- **Slippage:** difference between expected and actual execution price.
- **Spread:** may refer to bid–ask spread or a multi-leg strategy; define context.
- **Stop order:** conditional order that can become a market order when triggered; execution price is not guaranteed.
- **VaR / CVaR:** risk measures with assumptions and limitations; neither captures every extreme scenario.
- **Wallet:** software or hardware used to manage keys and sign transactions; it does not necessarily “contain” coins.
- **Yield:** income/return measure whose definition depends on the instrument.

## Additional glossary

- **Asset allocation:** division of a portfolio among asset classes.
- **Custody:** safeguarding or control of financial assets or private keys.
- **Data snooping:** repeatedly testing choices until an apparently strong result emerges by chance.
- **Drawdown:** decline from a prior portfolio peak.
- **Funding rate:** periodic payment mechanism used by some perpetual futures contracts.
- **Market impact:** price movement caused by executing an order.
- **Paper trading:** simulated trading without real-money execution.
- **Point-in-time data:** information that was actually available at the historical moment being tested.
- **Self-custody:** control of the private keys or signing authority by the user.
- **Survivorship bias:** excluding failed or delisted assets and thereby distorting historical results.
- **Walk-forward testing:** repeated evaluation on later unseen periods after calibrating on earlier data.

# TECHNICAL ANALYSIS — WHAT TO VIEW

1. **Moving averages (SMA/EMA)** — Trend smoothing and dynamic reference levels; lagging and prone to whipsaws.
2. **VWAP** — Volume-weighted average price; intraday execution benchmark, not universal support.
3. **RSI** — Momentum oscillator; overbought/oversold readings can persist in strong trends.
4. **MACD** — EMA-based momentum/trend indicator; lagging and sensitive to settings.
5. **ADX / +DI / -DI** — Trend-strength and directional-movement measures; ADX itself is not direction.
6. **Bollinger Bands** — Volatility bands around a moving average; a band touch is not automatically reversal.
7. **ATR** — Average true range; estimates volatility for stops/position sizing, not direction.
8. **Stochastic oscillator** — Compares close with recent range; can remain extreme in trends.
9. **CCI** — Deviation from average price; useful as a normalized momentum measure, not standalone.
10. **OBV** — Cumulative volume direction proxy; data quality and gap handling matter.
11. **Money Flow Index** — Volume-weighted momentum oscillator; divergence is only a clue.
12. **A/D line** — Volume-flow estimate based on close location in range; formula differs from accumulation/decline breadth.
13. **Parabolic SAR** — Trend-following stop-and-reversal dots; whipsaws in sideways markets.
14. **Ichimoku Cloud** — Trend, momentum, and support/resistance framework; needs coherent system rules.
15. **Pivot points** — Calculated reference levels; crowded levels can fail and definitions vary.
16. **Fibonacci retracement** — Potential retracement zones; not causal and often subjective.
17. **Donchian Channels** — Highest high/lowest low over lookback; useful for breakout systems.
18. **Keltner Channels** — ATR-based volatility channels; differs from standard-deviation bands.
19. **Supertrend** — ATR-based trend overlay; parameter-dependent and can lag.
20. **ROC / Momentum** — Rate of price change; compare across periods and assets carefully.
21. **Relative strength vs benchmark** — Measures relative performance, not the RSI oscillator.
22. **Advance/decline breadth** — Participation measure; index concentration can distort it.
23. **Put/call ratio** — Options activity sentiment proxy; contract type and volume/open-interest basis matter.
24. **Implied volatility** — Options market's volatility estimate, not a directional forecast.
25. **Historical volatility** — Realized past price variation; not a forecast by itself.
26. **Open interest** — Outstanding contracts; interpret with price, volume, expiry, and contract conventions.

Practical chart-review order:
1. Identify instrument, exchange, session, liquidity, and timeframe; check whether data are adjusted for splits/dividends.
2. Start with market structure: higher highs/lows, lower highs/lows, range, or transition.
3. Mark major support/resistance zones and prior swing highs/lows; treat them as zones, not exact lines.
4. Check volume and, where relevant, open interest, breadth, volatility, and event/news calendar.
5. Choose a small number of indicators with distinct jobs; avoid stacking redundant indicators.
6. Write the setup, entry trigger, invalidation/stop, target logic, position size, fees, slippage, and risk/reward before entering.
7. Test rules on out-of-sample data and different regimes; include realistic costs and avoid look-ahead/survivorship bias.
8. Keep a journal and review expectancy, drawdown, win/loss size, exposure, and rule adherence.


# FUNDAMENTALS — WHAT TO VIEW

1. **Business model** — What is sold, to whom, how recurring revenue works, and what drives unit economics.
2. **Industry structure** — Market size, growth, cyclicality, regulation, substitutes, and competitive intensity.
3. **Competitive advantage / moat** — Brand, network effects, switching costs, scale, cost advantage, patents, or distribution.
4. **Revenue growth** — Separate volume, price, mix, acquisitions, and one-off effects.
5. **Gross margin** — Revenue less cost of goods/services; trend can reveal pricing power or input pressure.
6. **Operating margin** — Operating profit relative to revenue; reflects core operating efficiency.
7. **Net profit margin** — Net income relative to revenue; check tax, interest, and exceptional items.
8. **EBITDA and EBIT** — Operating performance measures; reconcile adjustments and do not treat EBITDA as cash flow.
9. **EPS** — Earnings per share; inspect diluted shares and one-off gains/losses.
10. **Cash flow from operations** — Cash generated by operations; compare with reported earnings.
11. **Free cash flow** — Often CFO minus capital expenditure; definition varies by provider.
12. **Balance sheet** — Assets, liabilities, equity, liquidity, and financial resilience.
13. **Debt and net debt** — Debt less cash where appropriate; examine maturities, currency, and covenants.
14. **Interest coverage** — Ability to cover interest from operating earnings; definitions vary.
15. **Debt-to-equity** — Leverage ratio; compare within sector and accounting context.
16. **Working capital** — Current assets less current liabilities; study receivables, inventory, and payables.
17. **Receivables days** — Receivable collection speed; rising days may warrant investigation.
18. **Inventory days** — Inventory holding period; rising stock can signal weak demand or planned growth.
19. **Payables days** — Supplier payment timing; increases may support cash flow but stress suppliers.
20. **ROE** — Return on equity; assess leverage and buybacks behind the figure.
21. **ROIC** — Return on invested capital; compare with cost of capital and business cycle.
22. **ROA** — Return on assets; useful for asset-intensive business comparisons.
23. **Capital expenditure** — Maintenance versus growth capex; assess future cash requirements.
24. **Depreciation and amortization** — Accounting allocation of asset costs; compare with capex and asset life.
25. **Goodwill and intangibles** — Review acquisitions, impairment risk, and assumptions.
26. **Contingent liabilities** — Litigation, guarantees, tax disputes, and off-balance-sheet commitments.
27. **Auditor opinion** — Read qualifications, emphasis-of-matter paragraphs, and auditor changes.
28. **Related-party transactions** — Assess terms, conflicts, balances, and whether transactions are material.
29. **Promoter/founder ownership** — Track ownership, pledging, insider sales, and changes over time.
30. **Institutional ownership** — Review FII/DII/fund ownership as context, not a buy signal.
31. **Share dilution** — ESOPs, warrants, convertibles, and equity issuance can dilute per-share value.
32. **Buybacks and dividends** — Assess capital allocation, sustainability, and balance-sheet effects.
33. **P/E ratio** — Price relative to earnings; compare with growth, risk, and sector peers.
34. **Price-to-book** — Price relative to book equity; more useful for some financial/asset-heavy sectors.
35. **Price-to-sales** — Price relative to sales; useful when earnings are low/negative, but ignores margins.
36. **EV/EBITDA** — Enterprise value relative to EBITDA; check leases, debt, and capital intensity.
37. **PEG ratio** — P/E relative to expected growth; highly sensitive to growth estimates.
38. **Dividend yield and payout** — Dividend relative to price and earnings/cash flow; high yield can be a warning.
39. **DCF valuation** — Discount forecast free cash flows; highly sensitive to assumptions and terminal value.
40. **Comparable-company valuation** — Compare relevant peers with consistent definitions and business mix.
41. **Sum-of-the-parts** — Values business segments separately when one multiple is misleading.
42. **Management guidance** — Compare statements with delivered results and past guidance accuracy.
43. **Earnings call** — Look for quantified drivers, changed assumptions, and evasive answers.
44. **Quarterly results** — Compare sequentially and year-over-year; account for seasonality.
45. **Annual report** — Read business overview, risk factors, MD&A, statements, and notes.
46. **Cash conversion** — Compare cash flow with profit over multiple years.
47. **Cyclicality** — Normalize earnings through full business cycles rather than peak-year multiples.
48. **Macro sensitivity** — Interest rates, inflation, FX, commodity prices, and credit availability.
49. **Currency exposure** — Foreign-currency revenue, costs, debt, and hedging.
50. **Customer concentration** — Dependence on a few customers or contracts.
51. **Supplier concentration** — Dependence on scarce inputs or a small supplier base.
52. **Regulatory exposure** — Licences, compliance, litigation, policy changes, and penalties.
53. **ESG/material risks** — Assess financially material environmental, social, governance, and transition risks.
54. **IPO offer document** — Read use of proceeds, risk factors, promoter history, dilution, and related-party dealings.
55. **Mutual fund fundamentals** — Expense ratio, tracking error, portfolio overlap, turnover, mandate, and risk.
56. **ETF fundamentals** — Index methodology, liquidity, spread, tracking difference, and costs.
57. **Bond fundamentals** — Yield, duration, credit quality, covenants, liquidity, and reinvestment risk.
58. **REIT/InvIT fundamentals** — Cash distribution, leverage, occupancy/asset quality, sponsor, and debt maturities.
59. **Crypto/token fundamentals** — Token utility, supply/unlocks, governance, protocol fees, security, concentration, and custody risk.

| 1 | Revenue growth | `(Current revenue / Prior-period revenue) - 1`; separate organic growth, acquisitions, price, volume, FX and one-offs | Sustainable >15% annual growth with cash conversion and stable margins | 8–15% | 0–8% or volatile growth | Negative growth for 2+ years without a credible recovery plan | Persistent steep decline, especially with margin and cash-flow deterioration | Higher is generally better **only if profitable and cash-generative**; acquisition-led or credit-led growth can be poor quality |
| 2 | Gross margin | `Gross profit / Revenue × 100` | High or improving versus comparable peers; often >40% in differentiated businesses | Around peer median or steadily improving | Below peer median or falling | Sharp unexplained decline; roughly >5 percentage-point fall over a year deserves investigation | Structurally weak versus peers with no scale/turnaround explanation | Higher is generally better; compare by industry—retail, distribution, manufacturing and software have very different norms |
| 3 | Operating margin | `Operating profit / Revenue × 100`; state whether operating profit is EBIT or another definition | Top-quartile peer margin, stable/improving; >20% can be strong in many asset-light businesses | Above peer median and stable | Around peer median or volatile | Persistent decline, or materially below peers | Negative for a mature company without a credible temporary explanation | Higher is usually better if not created by underinvestment or accounting adjustments |
| 4 | Net profit margin | `Net income / Revenue × 100` | Strong versus peers and supported by operating cash flow | Positive and near/above peer median | Thin, volatile, or far below operating margin without explanation | Repeatedly near zero or falling | Persistent net losses in a mature business, unexplained exceptional gains masking losses | Higher is usually better, but check interest, tax, one-offs and non-operating gains |

| 6 | EBIT | Operating profit after depreciation and amortization | Positive, growing, and resilient through a cycle | Positive and broadly stable | Volatile or declining | Negative in a mature business | Persistent negative EBIT with no credible path to profitability | Higher is generally better; compare absolute EBIT only alongside revenue, assets and peers |

| 10 | Cash conversion | `CFO / Net income`; use multi-year totals if net income is positive | Generally >1.0 over a multi-year period | Around 0.8–1.0 | Around 0.5–0.8 or highly volatile | Below 0.5 for several years | Negative CFO despite sustained positive net income | Higher is usually better; one year can be distorted by working capital, tax, or seasonality. N/M if net income is zero/negative |
| 11 | Revenue quality | Check organic vs acquired growth, customer collections, returns, rebates, and contract terms | Recurring/contracted or repeat demand, transparent recognition, collections track sales | Mostly repeatable sales and ordinary terms | High customer/project concentration or volatile sales | Sales grow much faster than receivables; frequent extensions/returns | Aggressive recognition, channel stuffing allegations, unexplained receivables, restatements | More repeatable, collected revenue is better; growth alone is not enough |

## 3. Liquidity, leverage, debt service and working capital

| # | Metric | Formula / what to inspect | Best / strong | Good | Moderate / investigate | Risky | Bad / red flag | Higher or lower: interpretation |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | Current ratio | `Current assets / Current liabilities` | Often 1.5–2.5 for a conventional operating company, with good-quality current assets | 1.2–1.5 | 1.0–1.2 | 0.8–1.0 | <0.8 with weak cash generation or near-term maturities | Higher can improve short-term cover, but an extremely high ratio can mean idle cash, slow inventory or uncollected receivables. Not suitable as a standalone bank metric |
| 13 | Quick ratio | `(Cash + marketable securities + receivables) / Current liabilities`; definitions vary | Around 1.0 or higher for many conventional businesses | 0.8–1.0 | 0.6–0.8 | 0.4–0.6 | <0.4 with imminent obligations and weak CFO | Higher generally means better liquid cover; receivables quality matters |
| 14 | Cash ratio | `(Cash + cash equivalents + short-term liquid investments) / Current liabilities` | Strong cash buffer relative to near-term needs | Adequate for business volatility | Thin cash buffer | Little cash and limited committed facilities | Cannot meet near-term obligations without refinancing or asset sales | Higher offers liquidity, but cash held for operations/restricted cash must be distinguished |
| 15 | Debt-to-equity | `Total debt / Shareholders' equity`; state whether lease liabilities included | Low relative to peer norms; often <0.3 for non-financial companies | 0.3–0.7 | 0.7–1.0 | 1.0–2.0, depending on sector and cash stability | >2.0 can be severe for a conventional company, especially with weak cash flow | Lower is usually safer, but negative/very low equity makes the ratio misleading; banks/financial firms require sector-specific analysis |
| 16 | Net debt / EBITDA | `(Interest-bearing debt - eligible cash) / EBITDA`; use consistent lease treatment | Net cash or <1.0× | 1.0–2.0× | 2.0–3.0× | 3.0–4.0× | >4.0× often high risk for a typical non-financial business; >5× is especially concerning without stable contracted cash flow | Lower is safer; negative EBITDA makes this N/M, not “good” |
| 17 | Interest coverage | `EBIT / Interest expense` (some sources use EBITDA; label the definition) | >8× | 4–8× | 2–4× | 1–2× | <1×: operating earnings do not cover interest under this definition | Higher generally means more debt-service cushion; use EBIT-based coverage and cash-flow checks together |
| 18 | Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) | Cash available for debt service / scheduled principal + interest; definitions vary | >1.5× | 1.25–1.5× | 1.0–1.25× | 0.8–1.0× | <0.8× | Higher is safer; use the lender/covenant definition and assess forecast assumptions |
| 19 | Net debt | `Debt - cash and cash equivalents` (confirm eligible cash) | Net cash or low net debt with robust FCF | Manageable versus FCF and maturities | Rising debt but funded investment plan | Debt rises while FCF weakens | Refinancing dependence, covenant pressure, or debt growth with deteriorating operations | Lower is generally safer; absolute amount alone is meaningless without EBITDA, FCF, interest and maturity schedule |
| 20 | Working capital | `Current assets - Current liabilities`; inspect operating working capital separately | Funding cycle stable or improving without stressing suppliers | Normal for sector and growth | Increasing cash tied up in receivables/inventory | Working-capital needs grow faster than sales | Liquidity crisis, overdue payables, or repeated emergency borrowing | Positive is not automatically good; quality, turnover and cash conversion matter |
| 21 | Receivable days (DSO) | Common form: `Average trade receivables / Revenue × 365`; use credit sales if available | Stable or falling and better than peer norms | Near peer norm and consistent with terms | Rising modestly or volatile | Increase of ~15–25% year-over-year without a clear reason | Very high vs peers/terms, overdue balances, write-offs or sales growth unsupported by collections | Lower is often better, but payment terms and business model differ; rising DSO is a warning |
| 22 | Inventory days (DIO) | `Average inventory / Cost of goods sold × 365` | Stable/improving with no stock-outs | Normal for sector and product mix | Inventory grows faster than sales | Days rise ~15–25% year-over-year without planned stocking explanation | Obsolete stock, write-downs, falling sales and sharply rising days | Lower can be better, but too low may mean stock-outs; compare with seasonality and sector |
| 23 | Payables days (DPO) | `Average trade payables / Purchases or COGS × 365`; formula choice matters | Sustainable terms and no supplier stress | Consistent with negotiated terms | Rising reliance on supplier credit | Material rise without stronger bargaining power or clear seasonality | Overdue payables, supplier disputes, supply interruptions | Higher improves near-term cash only if contractual and sustainable; extreme increases can signal distress |
| 24 | Cash conversion cycle (CCC) | `DSO + DIO - DPO` | Short/negative where structurally possible and sustainable | Stable/improving vs peers | Long or lengthening cycle | Working capital consumes more cash each year | Severe deterioration plus liquidity stress | Lower is often better, but a negative CCC can be business-model-specific; never compare unrelated sectors blindly |
| 25 | Debt maturity profile | Review principal by year, floating/fixed rates, currency, covenants and refinancing | Staggered maturities, ample cash/committed facilities, covenant headroom | Manageable maturities with credible cash coverage | Large maturity in next 12–24 months | Reliance on refinancing or asset sales | Imminent maturity without funding, covenant breach or default risk | More liquidity and longer staggered maturities are safer; there is no single universal ratio |

## 4. Returns, efficiency and reinvestment

| # | Metric | Formula / what to inspect | Best / strong | Good | Moderate / investigate | Risky | Bad / red flag | Higher or lower: interpretation |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 26 | ROE | `Net income / Average shareholders' equity × 100` | >20% sustained with moderate leverage and clean earnings | 15–20% | 8–15% | <8% for a mature business, or high ROE driven mainly by leverage | Negative/very low ROE with deteriorating operations; denominator distorted by negative equity | Higher can be good, but debt, buybacks, one-offs and low equity can inflate it |
| 27 | ROIC | `NOPAT / Average invested capital × 100`; define invested capital consistently | Sustained ROIC > cost of capital by ~5 percentage points or more | ROIC > cost of capital | Near cost of capital (roughly within ±2 points) | Below cost of capital by >2 points for several years | Persistently negative returns on capital with no credible turnaround | Higher and above cost of capital is better; this is often more informative than ROE for operating businesses |
| 28 | ROA | `Net income / Average total assets × 100`; sector-specific | Strong versus peers and stable through cycle | Around/above peer median | Below peer median | Falling with asset growth or asset-quality deterioration | Losses and impairments indicate assets are not earning adequate returns | Higher is usually better; banks, insurers, asset-heavy firms and software companies differ materially |
| 29 | Asset turnover | `Revenue / Average total assets` | High/improving versus sector | Around/above peer median | Below peer median | Declining without a planned investment ramp | Assets expand but sales fail to follow over multiple years | Higher can indicate efficient asset use, but low-turnover businesses may have high margins |
| 30 | Capital expenditure intensity | `Capex / Revenue × 100`; distinguish maintenance and growth capex | Capex earns attractive incremental returns and is funded safely | Sustainable relative to cash generation | High or volatile capex needing explanation | Capex materially exceeds internal cash generation for years | Repeated projects overrun, weak returns, and debt/dilution rise | Neither high nor low is inherently good; evaluate necessity, returns and funding |
| 31 | Incremental ROIC | Change in NOPAT / change in invested capital over a suitable period; noisy measure | New capital earns well above cost of capital | Above cost of capital | Near cost of capital | Below cost of capital | Large investment with persistently negative incremental returns | Higher is better when measured consistently; can be distorted by acquisitions and cycle timing |
| 32 | Diluted share-count growth | `(Current diluted shares / Prior diluted shares) - 1` | Flat or declining share count without weakening the balance sheet | Low dilution, e.g. <1% annually | 1–3% annually | 3–5% annually without corresponding value creation | Persistent high dilution with poor per-share growth | Lower dilution is generally better; employee compensation and acquisitions can be justified if returns exceed cost |
| 33 | Dividend payout ratio | `Dividends / net income`; also check dividends / FCF | Sustainable payout with strong FCF; often 20–60% for mature non-financial firms | Covered by earnings and cash | 60–80% or volatile coverage | 80–100% with weak FCF | >100% sustained and funded by debt/asset sales | Lower gives flexibility; higher may be appropriate for mature, regulated or distribution-focused businesses |
| 34 | Dividend yield | `Annual dividend per share / Share price × 100` | Sustainable yield compared with peers and total-return needs | Reasonable and cash-covered | Unusually high vs peers | Very high yield, e.g. >1.5–2× sector median, without cash support | Yield appears high because price collapsed and dividend is likely unsustainable | Higher is not automatically better; an extreme yield may be a value trap |
| 35 | Buybacks | Review price paid, share-count reduction, funding source and management incentives | Repurchases below conservative intrinsic value, funded from surplus cash | Rational, transparent program | Neutral or small impact on per-share value | Debt-funded buybacks despite weak balance sheet | Buybacks at inflated prices while operations weaken or debt risk rises | No universal percentage threshold; judge price, funding, and share-count effect |

## 5. Valuation multiples — lower is not always better

**Valuation ratios must be compared with peers, growth, quality, interest rates, cyclicality and the company's own history.** A low multiple can indicate a bargain or a broken business; a high multiple can reflect quality or excessive optimism.

| # | Metric | Formula | Potentially attractive / best zone | Moderate / fair zone | Risky / expensive zone | Bad / red-flag zone | Interpretation |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 36 | P/E | Share price / diluted EPS | Below peer/history median **while** earnings quality and growth are sound; often <15× may screen cheaply in some mature sectors | Around peer/history median | >1.5× peer median without stronger growth/quality | Very high multiple with deteriorating growth, or negative earnings incorrectly treated as a low P/E | No universal “good P/E.” P/E is N/M for negative EPS and unreliable at cyclical earnings peaks |
| 37 | Forward P/E | Price / forecast next-12-month EPS | Discount to peers with credible forecasts | Near peers | Premium not justified by growth or quality | Forecasts require implausible earnings growth | Lower may be attractive only if forecasts are credible; analyst estimates can be wrong |
| 38 | Price-to-book (P/B) | Market capitalization / book equity | Below peer/history median if assets are sound and returns are adequate | Near peer median | High P/B with weak ROE | High P/B with poor returns or questionable asset values | <1× is not automatically cheap; use especially carefully for banks/financials and asset-heavy firms |
| 39 | Price-to-sales (P/S) | Market capitalization / annual revenue | Low versus peers with sustainable or improving margins | Around peer range | High multiple with weak margins | High multiple plus slowing growth and no path to profitability | Lower is not enough; two firms with identical sales can have very different margins and cash needs |
| 40 | EV/EBITDA | Enterprise value / EBITDA | Below peer/history range with sustainable EBITDA and manageable capex/debt | Near peer range | Premium unsupported by growth/quality | Extreme multiple with falling EBITDA, or EBITDA is heavily adjusted | Lower can be attractive, but capital intensity, leases, working capital and cyclicality matter |
| 41 | EV/EBIT | Enterprise value / EBIT | Discount to peers with sound assets and earnings | Near peers | Premium without superior returns | EBIT weak/negative or multiple is distorted by temporary peak/trough | Includes depreciation more directly than EV/EBITDA; still requires peer context |
| 42 | PEG | P/E / expected annual EPS growth rate; conventions vary | Roughly <1 may screen attractively if growth estimate is reliable | Around 1–2 | >2 may imply a high price for expected growth | Very high PEG with slowing growth or unreliable forecasts | A rough heuristic only; not meaningful with negative/near-zero growth or inconsistent growth units |
| 43 | FCF yield | FCF / market capitalization × 100; some use EV, so state denominator | >6–8% can be attractive for a stable business if FCF is sustainable | 3–6% | 0–3% | Negative FCF or a high yield caused by temporary/unsustainable cash flows | Higher can be attractive, but inspect capex cycles, working capital and whether cash flow is repeatable |
| 44 | Earnings yield | EPS / share price × 100; inverse of P/E for positive EPS | High vs comparable alternatives, with reliable normalized earnings | Similar to peers/alternatives | Low yield without sufficient growth | Low yield plus deteriorating earnings | Higher may offer more earnings per price paid; compare with bond yields and risk |
| 45 | EV/Sales | Enterprise value / revenue | Lower than comparable peers with similar margins and growth | Near peer range | High multiple without margin improvement | Extreme multiple with weak unit economics | Useful when earnings are negative, but debt and future margin potential must be examined |
| 46 | PEG using normalized earnings | P/E based on mid-cycle EPS / normalized growth | Reasonable versus peers after normalizing cycle | Near peers | Expensive after normalization | Valuation depends on peak-cycle earnings | Particularly useful for cyclical firms; normalize earnings over a full cycle |

## 6. Valuation methods and assumptions

| # | Item | What to test | Best / strong | Moderate / investigate | Risky / bad |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|
| 47 | Discounted cash flow (DCF) | Forecast FCF, discount rate, terminal growth, net debt, share count, dilution | Value remains above price across conservative/base scenarios; terminal value not dominating the result | Fair value depends heavily on one or two assumptions | Value only works with aggressive growth, falling discount rates, or unrealistic margins |
| 48 | Comparable-company valuation | Select genuinely comparable firms; align fiscal years and metric definitions | Valuation discount with equal/better quality and returns | Similar to peers | Premium without superior growth, returns, balance sheet or durability |
| 49 | Sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) | Value each segment separately; deduct corporate costs and net debt | Segment values supported by public evidence and conservative multiples | Wide range due to limited segment disclosure | Thesis relies on speculative values or ignores debt/corporate costs |
| 50 | Margin of safety | `(Estimated intrinsic value - Price) / Estimated intrinsic value` | Often seek 25–40%+ for uncertain estimates or cyclical firms | 10–25% depending on quality and uncertainty | <10% leaves little protection | Not a precise universal rule; required margin should rise with uncertainty and downside risk |
| 51 | Sensitivity analysis | Recalculate valuation across revenue, margin, discount rate and terminal assumptions | Reasonable value range under conservative assumptions | Wide but manageable range | Small assumption change destroys the investment case | If a small input change radically changes value, valuation uncertainty is high |
| 52 | Terminal value share of DCF | `Terminal value / Total enterprise value in DCF` | Lower reliance, or terminal assumptions well justified | 60–75% requires careful review | >75% means valuation is highly assumption-sensitive | Not an automatic rejection, but very high dependence on distant forecasts reduces confidence |
| 53 | Cost of capital | Estimate cost of equity and after-tax cost of debt; use appropriate capital weights | Forecast returns exceed cost of capital with a buffer | Returns near cost of capital | Returns below cost of capital | Growth destroys value when incremental returns remain below the relevant cost of capital |

## 7. Accounting quality, governance and disclosures

These are primarily **evidence-based tests**, not universal ratio thresholds. Do not assign a company a “good” score just because one ratio looks strong.

| # | Item | Good / best evidence | Moderate / investigate | Risky / bad evidence |
|---:|---|---|---|---|
| 54 | Annual report | Consistent statements, clear segment disclosures, reconciled KPIs, candid risk discussion | Complex notes or repeated changes in presentation | Missing/late reports, unexplained restatements, opaque disclosures |
| 55 | Auditor opinion | Unmodified opinion; still read key audit matters and qualifications | Emphasis-of-matter or significant uncertainty requiring explanation | Qualified, adverse or disclaimer opinion; auditor resignation with unresolved issues |
| 56 | Related-party transactions | Transparent, ordinary-course, arm's-length terms, proper approvals | Material transactions requiring benchmarking | Unexplained loans/guarantees, unusual terms, repeated transfers benefiting insiders |
| 57 | Promoter/founder ownership | Stable, aligned ownership with transparent transactions | Ownership changes with clear explanation | Sudden unexplained selling, opaque entities, governance concerns |
| 58 | Promoter pledge (India) | 0% pledged shares is generally the cleanest starting point | Low, stable pledge with clear reason and ample collateral | Rising pledge or pledge near high levels | High or rapidly rising pledge plus falling share price, margin calls or disclosure concerns |
| 59 | Institutional ownership | Diverse, disclosed ownership; changes understood in context | Concentrated ownership or rapid turnover | Crowded ownership used as sole investment thesis | More institutional ownership is not automatically good; ownership is context, not proof of quality |
| 60 | Share dilution | Transparent ESOP/issuance with value creation per share | Moderate dilution tied to growth/acquisition | Persistent dilution without per-share progress | Large issuance at unfavourable prices or unexplained dilution |
| 61 | Management guidance | Clear measurable guidance; history of meeting it or explaining misses | Frequent revisions with credible external causes | Repeated missed promises, changing definitions, selective metrics | Do not rely on promotional language without measurable delivery |
| 62 | Earnings call | Specific quantified answers, reconciliation of changes, discussion of downside risks | Vague answers or limited data | Evasive answers, contradictions with filings, unexplained KPI changes | Read transcript alongside filings; confidence/tone is not evidence by itself |
| 63 | Contingent liabilities | Material items disclosed, quantified, reasonably provided for | Outcome uncertain or amount material relative to equity/profit | Potential obligation could impair solvency or is poorly disclosed | Compare exposure with net worth, annual profit, cash and legal status; no universal safe amount |
| 64 | Regulatory exposure | Current licences, compliance systems, clean material enforcement history | Pending cases or policy dependence with disclosed mitigation | Material licence, litigation, environmental, tax or compliance uncertainty | A single unresolved issue can outweigh otherwise attractive ratios |
| 65 | Customer concentration | Diversified customers or long-term contracts with creditworthy buyers | Largest customer is material but manageable | One customer or small group drives a large share of revenue | Loss of one customer could threaten solvency or invalidate the business model |
| 66 | Supplier concentration | Multiple viable suppliers or secure long-term arrangements | Dependence on a key input with mitigation | Single-source dependency with weak bargaining power | Disruption could halt production and no realistic substitute exists |
| 67 | Macro / currency exposure | Stress-tested sensitivity and sensible hedging | Exposure disclosed but only partly mitigated | Large unhedged exposure relative to cash/profit | Adverse FX, rates, commodity or credit changes could threaten debt service |
| 68 | ESG / material risks | Material financial risks measured and governed; evidence, not slogans | Data incomplete or transition plan uncertain | Material safety, environmental, labour or governance exposures unmanaged | Assess financially material risk and cash impact; avoid treating a generic ESG score as a complete answer |

| Metric | Common formula | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth | `(Current revenue / Prior revenue) - 1` | Growth rate; investigate whether organic and cash-backed |
| Gross margin | `Gross profit / Revenue` | Pricing, product mix and direct-cost efficiency |
| Operating margin | `Operating profit / Revenue` | Core operating profitability |
| Net margin | `Net income / Revenue` | Bottom-line profitability after interest/tax/other items |
| Current ratio | `Current assets / Current liabilities` | Short-term balance-sheet coverage |

| Debt-to-equity | `Total debt / Shareholders' equity` | Balance-sheet leverage; misleading with negative equity |
| Net debt / EBITDA | `(Debt - eligible cash) / EBITDA` | Approximate leverage relative to earnings; N/M if EBITDA is negative |
| Interest coverage | `EBIT / Interest expense` | Earnings cushion over interest expense |
| ROE | `Net income / Average equity` | Return to equity holders; can be inflated by leverage |
| ROIC | `NOPAT / Average invested capital` | Operating return on capital invested |
| ROA | `Net income / Average total assets` | Profitability relative to assets |
| Operating cash conversion | `CFO / Net income` | Whether accounting profit converts into operating cash |
| Free cash flow | `CFO - capex` | Cash after capital investment, under this simplified definition |
| EPS | `Net income attributable to ordinary shareholders / Weighted-average diluted shares` | Earnings allocated per diluted share |
| P/E | `Price per share / Diluted EPS` | Price paid per unit of earnings; N/M for negative EPS |
| P/B | `Market capitalization / Book equity` | Price relative to accounting equity |

## 10. How to compare a company properly

1. **Identify the sector and business model first.** Do not compare a bank with a software firm, a retailer with a utility, or a REIT with a manufacturer using the same thresholds.
2. **Use consistent definitions.** Check whether debt includes leases, EBITDA is adjusted, cash is restricted, and EPS is diluted.
3. **Compare three ways:** company versus peers, company versus its own 5–10-year history, and company versus its full business cycle.
4. **Check trend, not only the latest value.** Improving from a weak base may be meaningful; a high current ratio at a cyclical peak may not be sustainable.
5. **Cross-check the income statement with the balance sheet and cash-flow statement.** Revenue/profit growth without collections or cash generation deserves scrutiny.
6. **Investigate extremes.** Very high ROE, very low P/E, very high dividend yield, very high growth, or unusually high margins may be real strengths—or signs of leverage, a cycle peak, a one-off, or a data problem.
7. **Use a scorecard only after reading disclosures.** Ratios cannot replace annual reports, notes, auditor opinions, management commentary, debt maturities, or regulatory filings.
8. **For India, verify primary sources** such as company filings on NSE/BSE, SEBI disclosures, annual reports, and relevant regulator publications. For global companies, use official exchange/regulator filings where possible.

Confirm that a resource is still active and check licensing, pricing, jurisdiction, data delay, and terms of use. ## How to learn a subject deeply

For every topic, use this order:

1. **Vocabulary:** define every term and identify the units, inputs, and outputs.
2. **Mechanics:** explain what the instrument, accounting measure, chart, model, or market process actually represents.
3. **Interpretation:** identify what conclusions the evidence can support—and what it cannot support.
4. **Worked examples:** reproduce calculations using a small, transparent dataset.
5. **Practical task:** produce a chart, spreadsheet, research note, model, or simulation.
6. **Failure cases:** investigate where the method gives a misleading answer.
7. **Validation:** compare with an independent source, baseline, benchmark, or out-of-sample period.
8. **Written analysis:** summarize the finding, assumptions, limitations, risks, and unresolved questions in plain language.

Do not skip the mechanics and validation steps in favor of memorizing signals.

## Fundamental analysis — a repeatable zero-to-advanced workflow

Fundamental analysis asks what a business does, how it earns and converts cash, how it is financed, what could impair it, and what assumptions are embedded in its market valuation. It does **not** produce a certain intrinsic value.

### Step 1 — Understand the business before the ratios

- Describe the product/service, customer, geography, distribution model, and revenue drivers.
- Identify whether revenue is recurring, transactional, cyclical, regulated, commodity-sensitive, or concentrated.
- Map suppliers, customers, competitors, substitutes, and barriers to entry.
- Read the business overview, risk factors, management discussion, segment notes, and auditor's report in official filings.
- Distinguish management claims from audited statements, contractual facts, and independently verifiable evidence.

### Step 2 — Read the three financial statements together

**Income statement:** revenue, cost of goods/services, gross profit, operating expenses, operating profit, interest, tax, and net income.

**Balance sheet:** cash, receivables, inventory, other assets, debt, payables, provisions, leases, and shareholders' equity.

**Cash-flow statement:** operating cash flow, capital expenditure, investing cash flows, financing cash flows, dividends, debt issuance/repayment, and changes in cash.

Reconcile the statements. Profit is not the same as cash generated. Rising receivables, inventory accumulation, capitalized costs, large one-off items, dilution, or repeated adjustments may warrant investigation rather than an automatic conclusion.

### Step 3 — Calculate and interpret ratios

Use consistent definitions, fiscal periods, currencies, and accounting bases. Compare a company with its own history and relevant peers; sector differences can make cross-sector comparisons misleading.

| Measure | Illustrative formula | What to investigate |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth | `(Current revenue / Prior revenue) - 1` | Organic growth, acquisitions, pricing, currency, and cyclicality |
| Gross margin | `Gross profit / Revenue` | Pricing power, product mix, input costs |
| Operating margin | `Operating profit / Revenue` | Operating leverage and expense discipline |
| Net margin | `Net income / Revenue` | Interest, tax, exceptional items, and accounting effects |
| Current ratio | `Current assets / Current liabilities` | Short-term liquidity, including asset quality |
| Debt-to-equity | `Debt / Equity` | Definition of debt, leases, negative/low equity, and industry norms |
| Interest coverage | `Operating earnings measure / Interest expense` | Earnings volatility and debt-service capacity |
| Return on equity | `Net income / Average equity` | Leverage, buybacks, and balance-sheet effects |
| Return on invested capital | `After-tax operating profit / Invested capital` | Capital intensity and returns relative to cost of capital |
| Operating cash conversion | `Operating cash flow / Net income` | Timing, working capital, and earnings quality |
| Free cash flow (common simplified form) | `Operating cash flow - Capital expenditure` | Maintenance vs. growth capex and business reinvestment |
| EPS | `Net income attributable to ordinary shareholders / Weighted-average diluted shares` | Dilution, buybacks, and one-off earnings |
| P/E | `Price per share / Earnings per share` | Earnings quality, growth, cyclicality, and comparability |
| EV/EBITDA | `Enterprise value / EBITDA` | Capital expenditure, leases, working capital, and debt |
| Dividend payout ratio | `Dividends / Relevant earnings measure` | Sustainability, cash coverage, and financing |
| Free-cash-flow yield | `Free cash flow / Market capitalization or enterprise value` | Use a clearly stated denominator and consistent definitions |

These formulas are starting conventions, not universal accounting rules. Explain the exact definition used in every report.

### Step 4 — Analyze quality and risk

- **Earnings quality:** compare profit with operating cash flow over multiple periods; investigate repeated adjustments and unusual accruals.
- **Balance-sheet risk:** review debt maturities, interest-rate exposure, refinancing, contingent liabilities, guarantees, and liquidity.
- **Dilution:** examine options, warrants, convertibles, employee compensation, and share-count changes.
- **Governance:** inspect auditor changes, related-party transactions, promoter/insider pledges where applicable, capital allocation, and disclosure consistency.
- **Business durability:** analyze competition, customer concentration, regulation, technology substitution, and bargaining power.
- **Cyclicality:** compare performance across different economic and commodity environments rather than extrapolating a peak year.
- **Evidence quality:** date every source, cite primary filings, and keep a list of unresolved questions.

### Step 5 — Value with scenarios, not a single magic number

Study comparable-company valuation, precedent transactions where appropriate, dividend discount models, and discounted cash flow (DCF). In a DCF, document forecast assumptions, discount rate, terminal value, reinvestment needs, and sensitivity to small changes. Compare bear/base/bull scenarios and state why each is plausible. A valuation model is conditional on its inputs and is not a precise prediction of market price.

### Step 6 — Write a complete company research note

Required sections:

1. Business description and revenue drivers.
2. Industry structure and competitive position.
3. Historical financial summary, ideally spanning a full business cycle where data exists.
4. Earnings and cash-flow quality.
5. Balance sheet, debt, dilution, and liquidity.
6. Management, governance, and capital allocation.
7. Valuation methods, assumptions, and scenario table.
8. Risks, counterarguments, and what would invalidate the thesis.
9. Catalysts and uncertainties, without treating catalysts as guaranteed.
10. Sources, publication dates, calculation definitions, and next review date.

**Practice task:** select a listed company, download official annual reports and exchange disclosures, build a five-year financial table, calculate key ratios, compare two or three suitable peers, and produce a sourced two-page research note. This is a research exercise, not a buy/sell recommendation.

## Technical analysis — chart reading from absolute beginner to advanced

Technical analysis studies price, volume, volatility, market structure, and sometimes positioning data. Chart formations are interpretations of historical observations; they do not prove future direction. The learner's job is to define a testable hypothesis, not to find a pattern after the outcome is already known.

### Step 1 — Understand the data behind every chart

- **Open, high, low, close (OHLC):** prices recorded over a defined bar interval.
- **Volume:** traded quantity under the data provider's definition; spot FX and some crypto/derivatives venues may have fragmented or venue-specific volume.
- **Timeframe:** the interval represented by each bar; changing the timeframe changes what is visible.
- **Adjusted versus unadjusted prices:** dividends, splits, and other corporate actions can distort long-term comparisons if data is not handled consistently.
- **Session and timezone:** market opens, closes, holidays, overnight sessions, and daylight-saving conventions affect bars and gaps.
- **Data source and timestamp:** record the provider, symbol, currency, adjustment policy, timezone, and whether data is real-time, delayed, end-of-day, or simulated.

Start with line charts for broad closes, OHLC bars for full price ranges, and candlesticks for open-to-close relationships. Use log scale when comparing long-term percentage moves across very different price levels; use linear scale when absolute price distances are the focus. Neither scale makes a chart inherently predictive.

### Step 2 — Learn price structure before indicators

1. Identify swing highs and swing lows using a rule defined in advance.
2. Describe an uptrend, downtrend, or range using those swings rather than a vague visual impression.
3. Mark support/resistance as **zones** where price previously reacted, not exact guaranteed barriers.
4. Distinguish a breakout from a brief intrabar breach; define whether confirmation means a close, a retest, a volume condition, or another measurable rule.
5. Record gaps, volatility expansion/contraction, and changes in trading range.
6. Note the broader market, sector, liquidity, and event context.
7. Write down an invalidation condition before assessing a setup.

A level that worked historically can fail. The more precisely a chart is drawn after the event, the greater the risk of hindsight bias.

### Step 3 — Chart and candlestick pattern families

**Reversal candidates:** double top/bottom, head-and-shoulders/inverse head-and-shoulders, rounding top/bottom, and selected wedge formations. These require a prior move and a defined confirmation rule; a shape alone is insufficient.

**Continuation candidates:** flags, pennants, channels, and some triangle formations. A continuation label is a hypothesis about a pause within a prior move, not a guarantee.

**Neutral or ambiguous formations:** rectangles, symmetrical triangles, and congested ranges may break in either direction. Plan for invalidation and false breakouts.

**Candlestick vocabulary:** doji, hammer, hanging man, shooting star, engulfing patterns, harami, piercing line, dark cloud cover, morning/evening star, and marubozu. Interpret candles in context: trend, location, bar size, liquidity, gap behavior, and the next bars matter. A candle name by itself is not a trading system.

**Gap categories:** common/range gaps, breakaway-like gaps, continuation/runaway-like gaps, and exhaustion-like gaps are traditional descriptions. Classification is often ambiguous, and gaps differ across markets and sessions.

**Other chart methods to learn after basics:** point-and-figure, Renko, Heikin-Ashi, volume-based charts, market profile, and anchored VWAP. Understand each transformation's calculation and what raw information it removes or smooths. Synthetic chart prices should not be mistaken for executable market prices.

### Step 4 — Indicators: know the input, formula, use, and failure mode

- **Moving averages (SMA/EMA):** smooth past prices; lag and whipsaw in ranges.
- **RSI:** momentum oscillator based on recent gains/losses; “overbought” is not an automatic sell signal, and strong trends can remain extreme.
- **MACD:** relationship between moving averages; lagging and parameter-dependent.
- **ATR:** estimates recent true-range volatility; not a directional forecast or guaranteed stop distance.
- **Bollinger Bands:** bands around a moving average using a dispersion measure; touches alone do not establish reversal.
- **ADX/DMI:** trend-strength/directional movement framework; strength and direction are distinct questions.
- **Stochastic oscillator:** compares close with a recent range; can remain extreme in persistent trends.
- **VWAP/anchored VWAP:** volume-weighted average price over a defined session or anchor; interpretation depends on data coverage and anchoring choice.
- **OBV and volume indicators:** transform volume and price information; results depend on the instrument's volume quality.
- **Fibonacci retracements/extensions:** commonly used reference levels based on selected swings; the selection is subjective unless algorithmically defined.
- **Breadth and advance/decline measures:** help describe participation across a market; constituent universe and index methodology matter.
- **Volatility measures:** realized volatility and implied volatility are different quantities and should not be treated as interchangeable.

For every indicator, write down: inputs, exact formula or implementation, lookback, scale, lag, what it measures, what it does not measure, likely failure conditions, and whether it adds information beyond price alone.

### Step 5 — A complete chart-reading checklist

Before forming a hypothesis, answer these questions in writing:

- What asset, venue, timeframe, session, and data source am I examining?
- Is the market trending, ranging, or transitioning? What objective rule supports that description?
- Where are the important swing points and reaction zones?
- What is volatility doing relative to its recent history?
- Is volume meaningful for this venue, and what does it actually measure?
- Is there a known event, earnings release, macro announcement, expiry, or liquidity issue?
- What exactly is the proposed pattern or signal, and what rule defines it?
- What would confirm it? What would invalidate it?
- What is the hypothetical entry, exit, stop/exit logic, and position-sizing rule?
- What are the spread, fees, slippage, gap, and liquidity assumptions?
- Is the same setup defined before the outcome, or only recognized after seeing it?
- What baseline will it be compared with?
- Has it been tested on data not used to design the rule?
- How many variations were tried before selecting the reported result?

### Step 6 — Turn a chart idea into a testable hypothesis

Example research template (not a strategy recommendation):

- **Hypothesis:** a specified event in price and volume is associated with a measurable subsequent return distribution.
- **Universe:** state the instruments and inclusion/exclusion rules.
- **Signal definition:** precise formulas and thresholds; avoid discretionary wording such as “looks strong.”
- **Timing:** specify when data becomes available and the earliest possible execution time to avoid look-ahead.
- **Outcome:** define return horizon and how dividends, corporate actions, and delistings are handled.
- **Costs:** include commissions, fees, spread, slippage, market impact where relevant, and financing/borrow costs if applicable.
- **Evaluation:** report sample size, average/median outcome, dispersion, drawdown, tail losses, turnover, and uncertainty—not just win rate.
- **Validation:** use chronological out-of-sample periods, walk-forward testing, robustness checks, and a benchmark.
- **Disclosure:** list parameter searches, failed tests, data limitations, and survivorship/look-ahead risks.

### Step 7 — Common chart-analysis mistakes

- Treating a pattern as a certainty or a price level as guaranteed support.
- Adding indicators until the historical chart looks convincing.
- Changing pattern definitions after seeing the outcome.
- Ignoring transaction costs, spreads, taxes, slippage, liquidity, and overnight gaps.
- Testing on a survivorship-biased universe or data adjusted with information unavailable at the time.
- Using future data inadvertently, including centered indicators or improperly aligned timestamps.
- Reporting only the best parameter combination after trying many.
- Confusing correlation with a causal explanation.
- Treating open interest, volume, put-call ratios, or social-media commentary as proof of institutional intent.
- Assuming a profitable backtest will survive live execution or a different market regime.

**Practice progression:** annotate 100 historical charts without placing trades; define one measurable setup; code it; test it against a simple baseline; include realistic costs; test out of sample; then run paper trading with risk limits and a trade log. This is a research workflow, not a promise of profitability.

## Options-chain analysis — what to read and what not to infer

For every observation, save the timestamp, underlying price, expiry, strike, call/put side, bid, ask, last traded price, volume, open interest, change in open interest, implied volatility, and Greeks when legitimately available.

- **Bid/ask and spread:** show quoted liquidity at a moment; last traded price may be stale or far from a current executable quote.
- **Volume:** contracts traded during the period; it does not identify who initiated every trade or reveal a trader's intent by itself.
- **Open interest (OI):** outstanding contracts under the exchange's reporting convention; every contract has both a long and a short side. OI alone does not reveal whether positions are bullish or bearish.
- **Change in OI:** change in outstanding contracts; it is not a direct measure of fresh bullish or bearish conviction without trade-level context.
- **Implied volatility (IV):** volatility input implied by an option-pricing model and market price; it depends on model assumptions, rates, dividends, and market conventions.
- **Delta:** local sensitivity of option value to the underlying under the model; not a guaranteed probability of expiring in the money.
- **Gamma:** change in delta as the underlying moves.
- **Theta:** model-based sensitivity to time passage, holding other variables fixed; real-world changes are not one-dimensional.
- **Vega:** sensitivity to a change in implied volatility.
- **Rho:** sensitivity to interest rates under the model.
- **Expiry and settlement:** confirm current contract specifications, settlement method, lot size, trading hours, and exercise/assignment rules from the official exchange.

Never present put-call ratios, OI “walls,” max-pain calculations, or a single snapshot as guaranteed support/resistance or proof of “operator activity.” These can be studied as descriptive variables, with clear definitions and empirical testing.

## Fundamental and technical analysis — how they differ and how to combine them

| Question | Fundamental analysis | Technical/quantitative market analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Main object | Business, financial condition, economics, valuation | Price, volume, volatility, order flow where available, and defined signals |
| Typical evidence | Filings, financial statements, business data, economic data | OHLCV, market data, indicators, execution and liquidity data |
| Main uncertainty | Future cash flows, competitive position, accounting quality, discount rates | Noise, changing regimes, execution, data quality, model overfit |
| Useful output | Company/asset research note, valuation ranges, risk scenarios | Explicit hypothesis, chart annotation, tested rule, risk and performance report |
| Frequent failure | Extrapolating growth, ignoring governance/debt, false precision in valuation | Hindsight pattern recognition, overfitting, ignoring costs, treating signals as certainty |

These methods can complement each other, but neither automatically validates the other. If combined, define the role of each input before testing and assess whether the combined approach improves out-of-sample results over simpler baselines.

## Stage 0 — Personal finance and financial literacy

Start here before learning to trade. A person who cannot distinguish savings, liquidity, debt cost, and investment risk cannot size market risk sensibly.

**Core resources:** [SEBI personal finance and investment education](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/), [SEBI financial education material](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/iematerial.html), [Investor.gov introduction to investing](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing), [Khan Academy finance](https://www.khanacademy.org/economics-finance-domain/core-finance). Related books: *The Psychology of Money*, *The Four Pillars of Investing*, *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*.

| Term | Meaning and where/when to use it | Formula / illustrative example | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income | Money received from salary, business, interest, dividends, rent, or other sources. Used to plan cash flow and affordability. | Monthly take-home income ₹40,000. | Use take-home cash for the household budget; distinguish gross income from net income. |
| Expenses | Money spent on essentials, discretionary items, debt service, taxes, and fees. | ₹24,000 expenses from ₹40,000 income leaves ₹16,000 before other goals. | Annual/irregular expenses should not disappear from the monthly budget. |
| Savings rate | Portion of income not spent; useful for tracking financial resilience. | `(Income − expenses) / income`; ₹16,000 / ₹40,000 = 40%. | A high rate is not useful if debt costs or essential liabilities are ignored. |
| Budgeting | A plan assigning income to needs, wants, savings, debt, and goals. | Build a monthly budget and compare actual spending with plan. | Revise using actual spending; do not treat a rule-of-thumb budget as universal. |
| Emergency fund | Liquid money reserved for unexpected costs or income interruption. | Example: essential costs ₹20,000/month; a 3–6 month planning range would be ₹60,000–₹1,20,000, adjusted to personal circumstances. | It is a planning example, not a fixed rule. Keep access and safety ahead of yield. |
| Debt and interest | Borrowed money creates contractual repayment and interest obligations. | Simple interest: `Principal × annual rate × time`. ₹10,000 at 12% for one year = ₹1,200 simple interest. | Compare effective rates, fees, compounding, penalties, and secured/unsecured terms. |
| Simple interest | Interest calculated on original principal only. | `I = P × r × t`. | Not the same as compound interest. |
| Compound interest | Interest accrues on principal plus previously accrued interest. | `FV = P(1+r)^n`. ₹10,000 at 8% for 2 years becomes ₹11,664 before tax/fees under annual compounding. | Returns are not guaranteed; investment returns fluctuate unlike a fixed-rate illustration. |
| Inflation / purchasing power | Inflation is a rise in the general price level; purchasing power is what money can buy. | Approximate real return: `(1 + nominal return)/(1 + inflation) − 1`. A 7% nominal return with 5% inflation is about 1.90% real before tax/fees. | Use an appropriate inflation measure; personal spending inflation can differ from headline CPI. |
| Time value of money | Money available now can be invested or used earlier than money received later. | `PV = FV/(1+r)^n`; `FV = PV(1+r)^n`. | The discount rate must reflect timing, risk, and the purpose of the calculation. |
| Financial goals | Specific amounts, deadlines, and priorities for future spending or obligations. | Goal = ₹2 lakh in 4 years; estimate contributions using a spreadsheet. | Do not assume a risky return for a near-term essential goal. |
| Risk tolerance | Psychological willingness to endure loss and volatility. | Usually assessed through scenario questions, not one formula. | Feeling comfortable in a questionnaire does not mean one can afford a loss. |
| Risk capacity | Financial ability to absorb loss without impairing essential goals. | Review income stability, liabilities, time horizon, and emergency reserves. | Tolerance and capacity can differ; the more restrictive constraint matters. |
| Liquidity | How quickly an asset can be converted to cash without a large price concession. | Cash is usually highly liquid; a thinly traded security may require a large spread. | Listed does not mean liquid. |
| Opportunity cost | Value of the best alternative forgone. | Choosing A with expected benefit ₹5,000 instead of B at ₹4,000 gives a simple ₹4,000 opportunity-cost comparison. | Compare alternatives on risk, timing, and uncertainty—not just headline returns. |
| Saving vs investing | Saving prioritizes capital access/stability; investing accepts risk for a potential return. | Compare a bank deposit with a diversified market investment by horizon, liquidity, tax, and risk. | Equity markets can fall sharply; they are not substitutes for emergency cash. |
| Diversification | Spreading exposure across assets, issuers, sectors, or risk factors. | Portfolio risk depends on weights, volatilities, and correlations, not the number of holdings alone. | Many funds can hold the same securities and provide little true diversification. |
| Nominal vs real return | Nominal return ignores inflation; real return adjusts for purchasing power. | Real return formula above. | Taxes and fees further reduce realized real return. |
| Taxes and transaction costs | Tax, brokerage, exchange fees, duties/levies, spreads, and other costs affect net results. | `Net result = gross result − costs − applicable taxes`. | Rules differ by jurisdiction, asset, holding period, and tax status; verify current official guidance. |
| Financial scams | Fraud using false promises, impersonation, fake apps, unauthorized advice, or manipulated markets. | No return formula; check registration, official domains, payment beneficiary, and withdrawal terms. | Guaranteed returns, urgency, secret signals, and requests for passwords/OTP are major warning signs. |
| Behavioral biases | Systematic decision errors such as overconfidence, loss aversion, anchoring, and herd behavior. | Keep a decision journal and compare planned actions with actual behavior. | A profitable outcome does not prove that the decision process was sound. |
| Personal financial plan | Integrated view of cash flow, debt, insurance, emergency reserves, goals, and investment horizon. | Write goals, due dates, required cash, and risk constraints. | Revisit after major changes; educational resources do not replace individual professional advice. |

**Stage 0 practice:** build a monthly budget; calculate savings rate, debt interest, compound growth, and inflation-adjusted returns; identify which funds must remain liquid. Do not start with live trading.

## Stage 1 — Economics and macroeconomic context

**Core resources:** [RBI](https://www.rbi.org.in/), [FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/), [World Bank Data](https://data.worldbank.org/), [IMF Data](https://www.imf.org/en/Data), [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/), [EIA](https://www.eia.gov/). Related books: *Economics* (Samuelson/Nordhaus), *Macroeconomics* (Mankiw), *Expected Returns* (Ilmanen).

| Term | Meaning and use in markets | Example / how to investigate | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microeconomics | How households, firms, and markets make choices under scarcity. Useful for pricing power, consumer demand, and competition. | Study whether a company can raise prices without losing customers. | A company-level conclusion does not alone predict its share price. |
| Macroeconomics | Economy-wide output, employment, inflation, credit, and policy. Used to frame sectors and asset classes. | Compare GDP growth, inflation, rates, and employment over time. | Markets price expectations, not just current data. |
| Supply and demand | Buyers and sellers jointly determine traded prices and quantities. | A supply disruption can raise a commodity price if demand does not fall enough. | Real markets include inventories, contracts, policy, expectations, and substitutes. |
| GDP / growth | GDP measures the value of final goods and services produced within an economy over a period. | Use official real GDP growth and compare with nominal GDP. | Revisions, base effects, informal activity, and per-capita differences matter. |
| Inflation | Sustained increase in a broad price index. | Read CPI components and compare monthly/yearly rates. | One item’s price change is not general inflation. |
| Interest rates | Cost of borrowing and reward for lending; rates affect discounting and financing. | Higher market yields can reduce the present value of distant cash flows, all else equal. | Impact depends on expectations, growth, risk premium, and the curve. |
| Employment and wages | Indicators of labor demand, income, costs, and economic capacity. | Strong wages may support consumption but also increase cost pressure. | Data lags and revisions; market reaction depends on expectations. |
| Monetary policy | Central-bank actions affecting policy rates, liquidity, and financial conditions. | Read RBI policy statements, decisions, minutes, and inflation outlook. | Policy changes influence markets through expectations and transmission lags. |
| Fiscal policy | Government spending, taxes, transfers, and borrowing. | Study a budget’s spending priorities and deficit financing. | Announcements may already be priced in; implementation differs from plans. |
| Central banks | Institutions responsible for monetary policy and/or financial-system functions. | Compare RBI, Federal Reserve, ECB, and Bank of Japan mandates. | Institutions have different mandates and operating frameworks. |
| Government borrowing | Debt issued to finance government needs; yields reflect rates, inflation, supply, and risk. | Track government bond yields and auction data. | A yield rise can have multiple causes; it is not a one-variable signal. |
| Exchange rates | Price of one currency in another. Affects importers, exporters, foreign holdings, and returns. | If a foreign asset rises 8% but its currency falls 5% versus home currency, home-currency return is approximately `(1.08 × 0.95) − 1 = 2.6%`. | Currency moves can dominate local asset returns. |
| Current account and trade | Cross-border flows of goods, services, income, and transfers; trade balance is one component. | Study official balance-of-payments releases and export/import composition. | A deficit or surplus alone does not determine currency direction. |
| Business cycles | Expansions, slowdowns, recessions, and recoveries in activity. | Compare output, jobs, credit, earnings, and inventories. | Cycles are identified imperfectly and often after data revisions. |
| Recession/recovery | Broad decline in activity / return toward expansion. | Use multiple official indicators rather than a single headline. | Country definitions differ; markets can turn before official confirmation. |
| Yield curve | Yields across maturities of similar-quality debt. | Plot 3-month, 2-year, and 10-year government yields and track changes. | Inversion is a risk indicator, not a timing device or certainty. |
| Credit conditions | Availability and cost of borrowing for households, firms, and governments. | Review credit spreads, lending standards, default rates, and bank data. | Market spreads can be distorted by liquidity and technical flows. |
| Economic indicators | Statistical releases such as CPI, PMI, GDP, jobs, production, and retail sales. | Maintain a calendar with release time, actual, consensus, prior, and revisions. | Consensus and revisions matter; avoid treating one release as definitive. |
| Commodity supply/demand | Production, inventories, transport, weather, substitution, and consumption shape commodity markets. | For energy, compare official inventories, production, and demand data. | Futures prices also reflect carrying costs, convenience yield, and risk premia. |
| Global capital flows | Cross-border investment and funding movements. | Compare portfolio flows, direct investment, dollar funding, and exchange rates. | Flows are measured with delay and may be revised. |

**How to read a macro release:** record the source and timestamp; compare actual with consensus and prior/revised values; ask which assets are directly exposed; identify what was already expected; then wait for corroborating evidence. Do not turn a single data point into a certain market forecast.

## Stage 2 — Mathematics, probability, and statistics for finance

**Core resources:** [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org/), [OpenStax](https://openstax.org/subjects/math), [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/), [NumPy](https://numpy.org/doc/), [pandas](https://pandas.pydata.org/docs/), [SciPy](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/), [statsmodels](https://www.statsmodels.org/). Related books: *Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives*, *Evidence-Based Technical Analysis*, *Expected Returns*.

| Concept | Definition / use | Formula or small example | Common trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Percentages and ratios | Normalize values to compare across sizes or time. | Growth = `(new − old)/old`; ₹100 to ₹110 = 10%. | A 50% fall requires a 100% gain to recover. |
| Algebra | Express unknowns and relationships. Used in payoff, valuation, and position sizing. | If `P/L = quantity × price change`, solve quantity for a target risk budget. | Confirm units and signs. |
| Logarithms / exponential growth | Logs turn multiplicative growth into additive changes; exponentials model compounding. | `ln(P_t/P_0)` is a log return. | Log returns are not identical to simple returns for large moves. |
| Time value of money | Discount or compound cash flows. | `PV = CF_t/(1+r)^t`; discount multiple future cash flows and add them. | Discount rate and timing assumptions drive valuation. |
| Probability | Numerical description of uncertainty under a defined model. | Probability must be between 0 and 1. | Model probability is not certainty; estimates depend on assumptions. |
| Expected value | Probability-weighted average outcome. | If a hypothetical payoff is +₹100 with probability 0.6 and −₹150 with probability 0.4, EV = `0.6×100 + 0.4×(-150) = ₹0`. | Positive EV does not prevent losses or account automatically for risk, costs, or changing probabilities. |
| Conditional probability | Probability of A given B. | `P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B)` when `P(B)>0`. | Conditioning on a selected sample can create bias. |
| Random variable | Numerical outcome of an uncertain process. | Daily return is a random variable in a model. | Returns may not follow a stable distribution. |
| Mean / median | Mean is arithmetic average; median is middle observation. | Returns `−20%, 5%, 10%`: mean −1.67%, median 5%. | Outliers can pull the mean; neither alone describes risk. |
| Variance / standard deviation | Measures dispersion around a mean; standard deviation is square root of variance. | Sample variance uses deviations from sample mean and an `n−1` denominator. | Volatility measures dispersion, not the full probability of loss. |
| Covariance / correlation | Covariance captures joint variation; correlation scales it to −1…+1. | Correlation = covariance divided by product of standard deviations. | Correlation changes; historical low correlation can break in stress. |
| Distributions | Models of possible outcomes, such as normal, t, or empirical distributions. | Plot a return histogram and compare with a normal curve. | Financial returns can be skewed and fat-tailed. |
| Sampling | Using a subset of observations to estimate a population/process. | Use chronological samples for time-series evaluation. | Non-random samples and missing delisted assets bias results. |
| Confidence interval | Range produced by a method with a stated long-run coverage property under assumptions. | Estimate a mean and its interval using an appropriate method. | It is not the probability that a fixed parameter lies in a particular frequentist interval. |
| Hypothesis testing | Procedure for assessing data compatibility with a null hypothesis. | State null, alternative, test, significance threshold, and sample before testing. | Multiple testing inflates false discoveries. |
| Regression | Estimates relationship between a response and explanatory variables. | `Y = α + βX + ε`; estimate beta and residuals. | Association is not causation; omitted variables and non-stationarity matter. |
| Statistical significance | Measures incompatibility with a null under a model, often summarized by a p-value. | Report estimate, uncertainty, sample size, and effect size together. | Statistical significance does not imply economic usefulness. |
| Time series | Observations ordered through time. | Plot level, returns, rolling mean, and rolling volatility. | Avoid random train/test splits that leak future information. |
| Simple vs log returns | Simple return `P_t/P_{t-1}−1`; log return `ln(P_t/P_{t-1})`. | Price 100→110: simple 10%, log ≈9.53%. | Use consistent conventions for aggregation and portfolio analysis. |
| Arithmetic vs geometric returns | Arithmetic mean averages periodic returns; geometric mean reflects compounded growth. | Geometric return per period = `(product(1+r_i))^(1/n)−1`. | Arithmetic mean usually exceeds geometric mean when returns vary. |
| Volatility calculation | Standard deviation of periodic returns; annualization depends on sampling frequency and assumptions. | Common approximation: daily return SD × `sqrt(252)` for trading-day data. | The 252 factor is a convention; clustered volatility and missing sessions matter. |

**Practice:** compute simple and log returns, annualized volatility, drawdown, correlation, and a confidence interval on a historical dataset. Document the data source, dates, adjustments, and whether returns include dividends.

## Stage 3 — How financial markets and trades work

**Core resources:** [SEBI investor education](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/iematerial.html), [NSE](https://www.nseindia.com/), [BSE](https://www.bseindia.com/), [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/), [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors), [CME Education](https://www.cmegroup.com/education.html). Related books: *Trading and Exchanges*, *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*.

| Term | Meaning / when it matters | Example / workflow | Risk or limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary vs secondary market | Primary issuance raises capital for an issuer; secondary trading transfers existing securities between investors. | IPO fresh issue vs a shareholder selling existing shares. | Offer-for-sale proceeds generally go to selling holders, not the company. |
| Exchange vs OTC | Exchange trading follows venue rules; OTC trades are negotiated through counterparties/dealers or networks. | Listed equity on NSE vs a negotiated OTC contract. | OTC terms, transparency, and counterparty risks vary. |
| Broker | Intermediary that routes/executes client orders and provides account services. | Verify registration and read the tariff sheet and contract notes. | Broker is not a bank; protect credentials and verify fund-transfer instructions. |
| Custodian | Holds assets and records ownership for clients/funds. | Institutional portfolio uses a custodian separate from its manager. | Custody model and segregation rules differ by jurisdiction. |
| Depository | Maintains securities in electronic/book-entry form through the relevant infrastructure. | In India, CDSL/NSDL infrastructure supports demat holdings. | A trading app display is not the only record; reconcile official statements. |
| Clearing corporation | Calculates obligations and manages clearing/settlement risk under venue rules. | Matches member obligations after trades. | Guarantees and protections are rule-specific, not absolute against every risk. |
| Settlement | Exchange of cash and securities/contract obligations after a trade. | Read the current exchange/clearing schedule for the instrument. | Settlement cycles and procedures change; do not assume all products settle identically. |
| Bid / ask | Best displayed buying price / selling price under a quote definition. | Bid ₹99, ask ₹100. | Quotes can be small, stale, or disappear before execution. |
| Bid-ask spread | Ask minus bid; implicit transaction cost for crossing the spread. | ₹100−₹99 = ₹1 spread. | Relative spread `spread/mid-price` helps compare instruments. |
| Market depth | Quantity available at different prices in the order book. | Compare available quantities near the best bid/ask. | Visible depth can be cancelled and may not represent all liquidity. |
| Liquidity | Ability to trade promptly with limited price impact. | A narrow spread and deep book often indicate better liquidity. | Liquidity can vanish during news or stress. |
| Market order | Prioritizes execution, not price. | Buy order may fill across several ask levels. | Execution price is not guaranteed. |
| Limit order | Sets the worst acceptable price, but execution is not guaranteed. | Buy limit ₹99 may not fill if ask stays ₹100. | Partial fills and missed trades are possible. |
| Stop order | Activates under a trigger rule; exact behavior depends on order type/venue. | A stop-market order can execute far from trigger after a gap. | Trigger price is not a guaranteed execution price; stop-limit can fail to fill. |
| Trading session | Defined venue hours and special sessions. | Verify official calendar, auction periods, holidays, and timezone. | Overnight/global news may affect next opening price. |
| Trading halt / circuit breaker | Rules temporarily pause or constrain trading in specified conditions. | Check official exchange notices and instrument-specific rules. | A halt does not guarantee a fair exit price after reopening. |
| Corporate action | Issuer event such as dividend, split, bonus, rights issue, merger, or buyback. | Adjust historical price series and check entitlement dates. | Unadjusted charts can create false gaps/returns. |
| Dividend / split | Dividend distributes cash/property; split changes share count and per-share price scale. | 2-for-1 split roughly doubles shares and halves per-share price, all else equal. | A split itself does not create economic value. |
| Index | Rule-based basket representing a market, sector, or factor. | Read index methodology, constituent rules, and rebalancing schedule. | Index composition changes and can introduce concentration. |
| Market capitalization | Price × shares outstanding under the provider's definition. | ₹200 share price × 10 crore shares = ₹2,000 crore. | Price and share count change; definitions of diluted shares vary. |
| Free-float market cap | Market cap based on shares considered available for public trading under index rules. | Used by many indices to weight constituents. | Free-float definitions are provider-specific. |
| Regulation / investor protection | Rules for conduct, disclosure, market integrity, complaints, and licensed intermediaries. | Read regulator and exchange sources before opening accounts or using advice. | Verify current law from primary official pages; beware impersonators. |

**Order-life-cycle exercise:** create a paper order, record quote, order type, timestamp, fill assumptions, fees, and settlement date. Compare the theoretical price with actual or historical bid/ask data. Never use an example to assume execution quality in a live market.

## Stage 4 — Long-term investing and portfolio construction

**Core resources:** [Investor.gov — asset allocation and diversification](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing), [AMFI](https://www.amfiindia.com/), [SEBI mutual-fund education](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/iematerial.html), [CFA Institute](https://www.cfainstitute.org/). Related books: *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing*, *Expected Returns*, *Modern Portfolio Theory and Investment Analysis*.

| Term | Meaning / where to use | Example / calculation | Important caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investment objective | Purpose, target, horizon, and constraints for investing. | Emergency reserve, home deposit, retirement, or education have different time horizons. | A target return is not guaranteed. |
| Risk-return trade-off | Potential return must be evaluated alongside possible losses and uncertainty. | Compare downside, drawdown, liquidity, and costs—not just average return. | Higher risk does not guarantee higher realized return. |
| Compounding | Returns on prior returns over time. | `FV=P(1+r)^n` under constant annual return assumption. | Actual market returns vary and sequence matters. |
| Diversification | Spreading exposures to reduce dependence on a single risk. | Mix assets whose returns are not perfectly correlated. | Correlations can rise during market stress. |
| Asset allocation | Portfolio weights across broad asset classes. | 60/40 is an illustrative equity/bond mix, not a universal recommendation. | Choose based on objectives, horizon, capacity, costs, and constraints. |
| Rebalancing | Restoring weights to target or rule-based ranges. | If target is 60% equities and drift reaches 70%, rebalance under a written rule. | Taxes, costs, liquidity, and market conditions affect implementation. |
| Passive vs active | Track a defined index vs seek outperformance through selection/timing. | Compare net-of-fee fund returns with the correct benchmark. | Active results are benchmark- and period-dependent. |
| Investment horizon | Time until money is needed. | Near-term funds usually require more attention to capital stability/liquidity. | Long horizon does not eliminate risk. |
| Systematic investing / dollar-cost averaging | Invest a fixed amount at regular intervals. | Invest ₹5,000 monthly regardless of price. | It does not guarantee better returns than investing a lump sum when funds are already available. |
| Lump-sum investing | Invest available capital at one time. | Compare with phased investing under realistic assumptions. | Outcome depends on market path and risk tolerance. |
| Behavioral finance | Studies how psychology affects financial decisions. | Pre-commit to a rebalance rule to reduce panic-driven decisions. | A checklist reduces but cannot eliminate bias. |
| Investment policy statement (IPS) | Written objectives, constraints, allocation, rebalancing, and review rules. | Define goals, horizon, liquidity needs, allowed assets, and risk limits. | Must be updated when circumstances change. |
| Benchmark | Relevant reference portfolio/index for evaluating performance. | Compare a domestic large-cap strategy with an appropriate total-return index. | A mismatched benchmark can make performance look misleading. |
| Costs and taxes | Expenses reduce net return and can compound over time. | Compare expense ratios and trading turnover over the intended horizon. | Current tax treatment depends on jurisdiction and instrument. |

**Practice:** write an IPS for a fictional investor; compare two diversified portfolios using return, volatility, maximum drawdown, liquidity, fees, and tax assumptions. Explain why historical outcomes do not determine future outcomes.

## Stage 5 — Indian equities and issuer research

**Core resources:** [NSE](https://www.nseindia.com/), [BSE](https://www.bseindia.com/), [SEBI reading material](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/iematerial.html), [NISM](https://www.nism.ac.in/), [CDSL](https://www.cdslindia.com/), [NSDL](https://nsdl.co.in/). Related books: *Financial Statement Analysis and Security Valuation*, *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits*.

| Term | Meaning and use | How to investigate | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listed company / share ownership | A share represents an ownership interest subject to the share class and legal terms. | Read the issuer's official filings, share-class details, and exchange disclosures. | Owning a share does not mean direct control over day-to-day operations. |
| Large/mid/small cap | Size classifications based on market capitalization and applicable local conventions. | Check current official index/industry classification definitions. | Do not apply one country's thresholds globally or assume small companies are always undervalued. |
| Sector classification | Groups businesses by economic activity. | Use exchange/index/industry definitions and understand diversified segments. | Companies within one sector can have very different economics. |
| Corporate announcement | Company disclosure about results, events, governance, or material developments. | Check exchange filing date/time and original document. | Headlines can omit caveats; read the full disclosure. |
| Annual report | Yearly financial statements, management discussion, governance disclosures, and notes. | Read auditor's report, statements, notes, related-party transactions, and segment information. | Marketing sections do not replace audited data. |
| Quarterly results | Periodic financial performance and disclosures. | Compare with prior quarter/year and expected seasonality. | Quarter-on-quarter comparisons can be misleading for seasonal businesses. |
| Dividend | Distribution to eligible shareholders, subject to declaration and rules. | Review amount, record/ex-date, payout, cash flow, and tax. | Dividend is not free money; price can adjust and sustainability matters. |
| Rights issue | Existing holders may be offered rights to subscribe to additional shares. | Read official terms, ratio, price, timetable, and rights trading details. | Non-participation may dilute ownership; terms differ. |
| Bonus issue | Additional shares distributed under issuer terms. | Check ratio and adjusted share count/price history. | It does not by itself increase total economic value. |
| Stock split | Changes share units and per-share price scale. | Adjust per-share historical data and share count. | Lower nominal share price does not mean cheaper valuation. |
| Buyback | Company repurchases shares under a stated process. | Examine price, funding, tender/open-market method, tax, and share-count effect. | Buybacks can be value-accretive or wasteful depending on price and funding. |
| Shareholding pattern | Reported ownership by categories under applicable rules. | Compare periods; review promoter/institutional/public holdings. | Categories alone do not reveal future buying or selling intent. |
| Promoter holdings / pledges | Ownership and shares pledged as collateral, where relevant. | Check official filings and changes in pledge levels. | A pledge is a risk signal to investigate, not an automatic sell signal. |
| Related-party transactions | Transactions involving entities or people connected to the company. | Read notes, approvals, terms, balances, and governance disclosures. | Not every related-party transaction is improper; terms and transparency matter. |
| Corporate governance | Systems of oversight, accountability, controls, and shareholder treatment. | Review audit qualifications, board composition, disclosures, and capital allocation. | Governance cannot be judged from one metric alone. |
| Trading/settlement costs | Brokerage, exchange fees, statutory levies, spread, slippage, and other applicable costs. | Use current broker tariff sheets and official rules; calculate round-trip costs. | Charges and taxes can change; verify for your transaction and tax status. |
| Investor grievances | Formal process for complaints against intermediaries/issuers. | Start with official broker/exchange/regulator complaint channels. | Preserve contract notes, statements, ticket numbers, and correspondence. |

## Stage 6 — Fundamental analysis and financial statements

**Primary resources:** [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/), [CFA Institute — Financial Statement Analysis](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/introduction-financial-statement-analysis), [CFA Institute — Financial Analysis Techniques](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/financial-analysis-techniques), [Damodaran Online](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/), [AccountingCoach](https://www.accountingcoach.com/). Related books: *Financial Intelligence*, *Financial Statement Analysis and Security Valuation*, *Financial Shenanigans*.

### Read the statements in this order

1. **Business model:** how the company earns money and what drives price, volume, costs, retention, and capital needs.
2. **Income statement:** revenue, costs, operating profit, interest, tax, and earnings over a period.
3. **Balance sheet:** assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time.
4. **Cash-flow statement:** cash generated/used in operations, investing, and financing.
5. **Footnotes and audit report:** accounting policies, estimates, debt terms, leases, contingencies, related parties, segments, and qualifications.
6. **Peer and cycle comparison:** compare consistent definitions across time and relevant competitors.
7. **Valuation:** connect business performance to cash flows and market price with explicit assumptions.

| Term / measure | Meaning / formula | How and when to use it | Example / warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income statement | Reports income and expenses over a period. | Use for growth, profitability, expense structure, and EPS. | Accounting profit is not cash flow. |
| Balance sheet | Reports assets, liabilities, and equity at a date. | Use for liquidity, leverage, solvency, and capital structure. | Historical cost and estimates affect reported values. |
| Cash-flow statement | Reports cash from operating, investing, and financing activities. | Check whether reported earnings convert to cash and how capex is funded. | Cash flow can be temporarily boosted by working-capital changes. |
| Revenue growth | `(Current revenue / prior-period revenue) − 1`. | Compare several years, peers, segments, volume, price, and acquisitions. | Growth from acquisitions or one-off contracts may not be repeatable. |
| Gross margin | `Gross profit / revenue`. | Investigate product mix, input costs, pricing, and scale. | Industry definitions of gross profit differ. |
| Operating margin | `Operating profit / revenue`. | Evaluate operating efficiency and operating leverage. | Adjusted profit may exclude recurring costs. |
| Net margin | `Net income / revenue`. | Evaluate final accounting profitability after interest and tax. | One-off gains/losses and capital structure matter. |
| Working capital | Often `current assets − current liabilities`; operating working capital has narrower definitions. | Track receivables, inventory, payables, and cash conversion. | High working capital can reflect growth or poor collection; context matters. |
| Capital expenditure (CapEx) | Cash spent acquiring/maintaining long-lived assets. | Examine cash-flow statement and notes; separate maintenance/growth capex where possible. | Accounting classification and asset intensity differ. |
| Free cash flow | Common simplified form: `operating cash flow − CapEx`. | Used in DCF and to assess capacity for debt service, reinvestment, dividends, or buybacks. | Define CapEx and cash-flow source consistently. |
| Debt / interest coverage | Leverage compares debt with equity, earnings, or cash flow; interest coverage compares an earnings measure with interest expense. | Examine maturities, rates, covenants, leases, refinancing, and cyclical lows. | No single ratio fits every sector; specify numerator and denominator. |
| ROE | Commonly `net income / average shareholders' equity`. | Evaluate returns to equity holders alongside leverage and buybacks. | High ROE may be caused by very low equity or high leverage. |
| ROIC | Commonly `NOPAT / invested capital`, with definitions disclosed. | Compare business returns with cost of capital and peers. | Invested-capital and NOPAT definitions vary; be consistent. |
| EPS | Earnings attributable to ordinary shareholders divided by weighted-average shares; diluted EPS accounts for dilutive instruments under applicable rules. | Track earnings per share and dilution over time. | EPS can rise due to buybacks even when total earnings stagnate. |
| Dilution | Increase in share count that reduces each existing share's ownership/earnings claim, all else equal. | Review options, warrants, convertibles, employee awards, and share issuance. | Not every issuance is bad; assess proceeds and expected return on capital. |
| Cash conversion | Often operating cash flow divided by net income over a chosen period. | Compare earnings with cash generated across several periods. | Working capital and seasonality can distort one period. |
| Accounting quality | How faithfully reported numbers represent underlying economics. | Read policies, estimates, audit qualifications, unusual adjustments, and restatements. | Red flags require investigation; a single ratio does not prove fraud. |
| Earnings quality | Sustainability and cash backing of reported earnings. | Compare recurring operating performance with adjusted and reported earnings. | Management-adjusted metrics may omit recurring expenses. |
| Segment reporting | Business results by operating segment under reporting rules. | Identify which segments drive growth, margins, capital needs, and risk. | Segment allocations and transfer pricing can limit comparability. |
| Competitive advantage | Factors supporting durable returns, such as switching costs, scale, network effects, brands, or cost advantages. | Test with margins, retention, pricing, market share, and reinvestment returns. | A narrative moat must be supported by evidence. |
| Industry structure | Rivalry, buyer/supplier power, entry barriers, substitutes, and regulation. | Use when comparing margins and the durability of returns. | Industry labels alone do not capture a company's position. |
| Corporate governance | Oversight, incentives, controls, disclosure, and shareholder treatment. | Read board/audit details, related-party notes, remuneration, and capital allocation. | Formal compliance does not guarantee good economic decisions. |
| Management incentives | Pay and ownership arrangements that influence decisions. | Compare compensation metrics with long-term value creation and dilution. | Incentive structures can encourage short-term targets or excessive risk. |
| Financial statement footnotes | Explanations and detailed disclosures supporting statement line items. | Read debt, leases, revenue recognition, contingencies, pensions, related parties, segments, and accounting changes. | Material risks often appear in notes rather than headline ratios. |

### Example: a compact fundamental-analysis calculation

Suppose a hypothetical company reports revenue of ₹1,000 crore in Year 1 and ₹1,150 crore in Year 2.

- Revenue growth = `(1,150 / 1,000) − 1 = 15%`.
- If operating profit rises from ₹120 crore to ₹138 crore, operating margin is 12% in both years.
- If operating cash flow is ₹90 crore and CapEx is ₹35 crore, simplified free cash flow is ₹55 crore.
- These facts alone do **not** prove the business is attractive. Next check debt, dilution, working capital, segment results, accounting notes, competition, valuation, and risks.

### Financial statement footnote checklist

- Revenue recognition policy and contract assets/liabilities.
- Debt maturity schedule, interest rates, covenants, and security.
- Lease liabilities and commitments.
- Contingent liabilities, litigation, guarantees, and tax disputes.
- Related-party transactions and balances.
- Segment definitions and changes.
- Goodwill, impairment tests, and intangible assets.
- Share-based compensation, options, convertibles, and diluted share count.
- Auditor opinion, emphasis-of-matter paragraphs, key audit matters where applicable, and auditor changes.
- Accounting-policy changes, estimates, restatements, and non-recurring items.

## Stage 7 — Valuation: from ratios to a reasoned range

**Core resources:** [Damodaran Online](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/), [CFA Institute valuation/financial analysis resources](https://www.cfainstitute.org/), [SEC company filings](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/). Related books: *Investment Valuation*, *The Little Book of Valuation*, *Valuation: Measuring and Managing the Value of Companies*.

| Method / term | Formula or meaning | Use and example | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| P/E | `share price / earnings per share`. | Compare profitable firms with similar growth, risk, and accounting. | Meaningless or unstable with negative/abnormally low earnings; cyclical peak earnings distort it. |
| P/B | `market value of equity / book value of equity`. | May be useful for some financial firms and asset-heavy businesses. | Intangibles and accounting choices affect book value; not equally useful for all sectors. |
| EV/EBITDA | `enterprise value / EBITDA`, where EV usually includes equity value plus debt and other claims less cash, per stated definition. | Compare operating businesses with similar economics. | EBITDA excludes CapEx, working capital, interest, and tax; definitions of EV differ. |
| EV/Sales | `enterprise value / revenue`. | Sometimes used for firms with low/negative earnings; inspect gross margin and path to profitability. | Revenue without margins or cash generation can be misleading. |
| Dividend yield | `annual dividend per share / share price`. | Assess cash income relative to price and compare payout sustainability. | A high yield can reflect a falling price or an unsustainable payout. |
| Free-cash-flow yield | Defined FCF divided by market cap or EV; state which denominator. | Compare cash generation with price. | FCF is sensitive to capex and working capital definitions. |
| DCF | Present value of forecast cash flows plus terminal value, discounted at a suitable rate. | Forecast revenue, margins, reinvestment, taxes, and cash flows; calculate a range. | Small changes in long-term growth/discount rates can produce large value changes. |
| Dividend discount model | Present value of expected future dividends. | Useful when dividends are meaningful and forecastable. | Less useful for firms with no meaningful dividend policy. |
| Comparable-company analysis | Compare valuation multiples of selected peers. | Select peers by business model, growth, margins, risk, and accounting. | Peer sets and current market multiples may be imperfect. |
| Precedent transactions | Use valuation metrics from comparable acquisitions. | Useful in transaction and corporate-finance work. | Control premiums, synergies, cycle, and deal terms differ. |
| Cost of equity | Required return for equity risk under the chosen model. | Often estimated using a risk-free rate plus risk-premium assumptions. | Inputs are uncertain; beta and premiums are model-dependent. |
| WACC | Weighted average cost of capital across financing sources under defined market-value weights and tax assumptions. | Discount unlevered operating cash flows when assumptions fit. | Do not mismatch cash flows and discount rates; capital structure can change. |
| Terminal value | Value assigned to cash flows beyond explicit forecast horizon. | Common perpetuity-growth form: `TV = FCF_(n+1)/(WACC−g)` when assumptions are appropriate and `WACC > g`. | Terminal value can dominate DCF; growth must be economically plausible. |
| Margin of safety | Gap between estimated value and purchase price under an explicit framework. | Test whether a conclusion survives pessimistic assumptions. | Not protection against a flawed model or permanent business impairment. |
| Sensitivity analysis | Change one or more inputs to show model dependence. | Vary growth, margins, WACC, terminal growth, and currency. | Inputs may be correlated; one-at-a-time sensitivity can miss joint effects. |
| Scenario analysis | Coherent bear/base/bull or alternative futures. | State assumptions and consequences for earnings, cash, and value. | Scenarios are not exhaustive and probabilities may be unknown. |
| Growth assumptions | Forecast of revenue, margins, reinvestment, and cash flow. | Tie forecasts to market size, capacity, pricing, and historical performance. | Extrapolating recent growth indefinitely is a frequent error. |
| Capital allocation | Decisions about reinvestment, acquisitions, debt, dividends, and buybacks. | Evaluate incremental returns and financing consequences. | A buyback or acquisition is not inherently value-creating. |
| Valuation limitations | Model outputs depend on data, assumptions, and market conditions. | Report ranges, sensitivities, source dates, and what would invalidate the model. | A valuation estimate is not a price forecast or guaranteed margin of safety. |

**DCF sequence:** select the cash-flow definition → forecast revenue and margins → forecast taxes and reinvestment → estimate free cash flow → choose a consistent discount rate → calculate terminal value → discount cash flows → test scenarios and sensitivities → compare with market value → write risks and limitations.

## Stage 8 — IPO research and application mechanics

**Core resources:** [SEBI Investor Education Material — primary market and IPOs](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/iematerial.html), [NSE](https://www.nseindia.com/), [BSE](https://www.bseindia.com/), [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/). Related books: *Investment Valuation*, *Financial Statement Analysis and Security Valuation*.

| Term | Meaning / use | What to check | Important caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| IPO | Initial public offering of shares to public investors under applicable rules. | Offer document, company history, ownership, risks, valuation, and use of proceeds. | An IPO is not inherently a bargain. |
| Primary vs secondary sale | New shares raise money for issuer; offer-for-sale sells existing holders' shares. | Identify split of fresh issue and selling shareholder proceeds. | The company may receive only the fresh-issue portion, after expenses. |
| Draft/final offer document / prospectus | Formal disclosures about issuer, offer, finances, ownership, risks, and terms. | Read business, financial statements, risk factors, related parties, litigations, and capital structure. | Use the latest official filed document, not only summaries. |
| Business model / use of proceeds | How the issuer earns money and how offer funds are planned to be used. | Compare stated proceeds use with debt, capex, working capital, and expansion needs. | Plans may change; monitor post-listing disclosures. |
| Promoter/shareholder holdings | Ownership before and after the offer. | Check dilution, selling holders, control, lock-ins, and related parties. | High or low promoter holding alone is not a sufficient conclusion. |
| Financial history | Historical revenue, profit, cash flow, debt, and key metrics. | Check audited periods, comparability, one-off items, and segment changes. | Short history or unusual growth can increase uncertainty. |
| Risk factors / litigation | Disclosed business, legal, operational, and financial risks. | Rank risks by likelihood, impact, and potential financial consequence. | Do not skip the long-form risk section. |
| Valuation / peer comparison | Compare proposed valuation with peers and issuer's own economics. | Normalize margins, growth, leverage, and share count; consider future dilution. | Peer selection and forward assumptions are judgment-heavy. |
| Underwriters / book building | Intermediaries and process used to market/allocate an offer under applicable rules. | Read official process, price band, allocation categories, and timetable. | Oversubscription is not proof of value or future returns. |
| Price band / lot size | Official bid range and minimum application quantity under offer rules. | Use current offer document and official exchange/registrar details. | Rules differ by category and may change. |
| Subscription data | Applications/bids relative to shares available in categories. | Use official exchange updates with timestamp and category breakdown. | Subscription can reflect allocation mechanics, not fundamental quality. |
| Allotment | Allocation of shares under applicable rules. | Check official registrar/exchange/broker channel. | Avoid fake allotment links and requests for OTP/password. |
| ASBA/application | Application process where funds are blocked under the applicable system rather than transferred as an informal payment. | Follow official bank/broker/exchange process and current rules. | Never send money to an unofficial account or UPI ID. |
| Listing-day move | First-session price change relative to issue price. | Record market context, liquidity, float, and price discovery. | A listing gain/loss is not proof of long-term value. |
| Lock-in | Restrictions on sale for specified holders/periods under rules and offer terms. | Read exact categories and dates in offer documents. | Lock-in expiry can affect supply but does not guarantee price direction. |
| Post-listing disclosures | Ongoing financial, governance, and material-event reporting. | Continue reviewing results and exchange filings. | IPO diligence does not end at listing. |
| SME IPO risks | Smaller-company offerings may have lower liquidity, concentrated ownership, limited history, and other specific risks. | Read official exchange/regulator advisories, market-making and lot rules, financials, and risks. | Low float and thin trading can amplify price moves. |

**IPO research deliverable:** a two-page note with the offer structure, issuer economics, financial trend, risk factors, use of proceeds, ownership changes, peer valuation, downside scenarios, and source links. Never use subscription counts or unofficial “GMP” chatter as a substitute for filed disclosures.

**Core resources:** [AMFI](https://www.amfiindia.com/), [SEBI Investor Education](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/iematerial.html), [RBI Retail Direct](https://rbiretaildirect.org.in/), [CME Education](https://www.cmegroup.com/education.html), [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/). Related books: *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing*, *Modern Portfolio Theory and Investment Analysis*, *Expected Returns*.

| Term | Meaning / use | Formula / example | What to verify |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mutual fund | Pooled vehicle investing under a stated mandate. | Review mandate, holdings, costs, risk, and benchmark. | Scheme documents, fees, liquidity, tax, and regulatory disclosures. |
| Index fund | Fund designed to track an index. | Compare total-return index and fund returns over matched dates. | Tracking difference, costs, replication, and benchmark version. |
| ETF | Exchange-traded fund whose units trade during market hours. | Compare NAV/iNAV, exchange price, spread, and liquidity. | Market price can differ from NAV; spreads and trading volumes matter. |
| Active/passive management | Security selection/timing vs index tracking. | Compare net returns with an appropriate benchmark over multiple periods. | Survivorship and benchmark selection can distort comparisons. |
| Expense ratio | Ongoing fund expenses expressed as a proportion of assets under the stated convention. | Approximate annual drag = expense ratio × average assets. | Additional transaction costs and taxes may apply. |
| Tracking error | Volatility of fund returns relative to benchmark returns, under a stated calculation. | SD of periodic active returns. | Distinguish tracking error from average tracking difference. |
| Tracking difference | Fund return minus benchmark return over a period. | If fund returns 9.2% and benchmark 9.8%, difference is −0.6 percentage points. | Ensure both returns use comparable dates and total-return treatment. |
| NAV | Per-unit net asset value calculated under fund rules. | `(assets − liabilities)/units outstanding`. | ETF trading price can diverge from NAV. |
| Index methodology | Rules for constituent eligibility, weighting, rebalancing, and corporate actions. | Read official provider methodology before interpreting an index. | Index names alone do not explain concentration or sector exposures. |
| Weighting method | Market-cap, free-float, equal, price, or other rule used to weight constituents. | Calculate each holding's portfolio weight. | Weighting rules shape sector and stock concentration. |
| Rebalancing | Updating portfolio/index weights to target or rule. | Compare target vs actual weights and transaction costs. | Turnover creates costs and potential tax consequences. |
| Replication | Full replication, sampling, or synthetic methods to track a benchmark. | Review fund documents and holdings. | Derivatives/counterparty risk may apply to synthetic replication. |
| Liquidity / spread | Ability to trade fund units and cost of crossing quotes. | Calculate relative spread `(ask−bid)/mid`. | Low screen volume does not always equal low liquidity, but spreads still matter. |
| Holdings / overlap | Assets held and similarity between funds. | Compare top holdings and aggregate overlap. | Several funds may duplicate the same underlying exposures. |
| REIT / InvIT | Listed trusts holding real estate or infrastructure assets under relevant rules. | Review distributions, leverage, occupancy/contracts, debt, and asset concentration. | Interest rates, regulation, tenant/offtaker concentration, and valuation matter. |
| Bond coupon / yield | Coupon is contractual interest; yield relates cash flows to price under a convention. | Price and yield generally move inversely for a plain fixed-rate bond. | Yield-to-maturity assumes cash-flow and reinvestment conventions; not a guaranteed realized return. |
| Duration / convexity | Measures interest-rate sensitivity and curvature of bond-price response. | Approximate percentage price change ≈ `−modified duration × yield change`, for small moves. | Approximation excludes spread changes and becomes less accurate for large moves. |
| Credit spread / default | Extra yield over a reference for credit/liquidity risks; default means failure to meet obligations. | Compare issuer spread, rating, cash flows, and debt maturity. | Ratings can lag; higher yield signals risk, not free extra return. |

## Stage 10 — Trading process, costs, and risk per trade

**Core resources:** [SEBI derivatives and safety education](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/iematerial.html), [NSE](https://www.nseindia.com/), [CME Education](https://www.cmegroup.com/education.html), [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors). Related books: *Trading and Exchanges*, *Evidence-Based Technical Analysis*, *Trading Systems and Methods*.

| Term | Meaning / formula | Use | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investing vs trading | Investing typically focuses on ownership/long-horizon objectives; trading focuses on defined price/risk opportunities over a shorter horizon, though boundaries overlap. | Choose method based on objectives, costs, and evidence. | Trading more frequently does not inherently improve results. |
| Trade plan | Written hypothesis, setup, entry/exit rules, sizing, costs, and invalidation. | Write before entry or simulation. | A plan is only useful if rules are measurable and followed. |
| Slippage | Difference between assumed/decision price and actual execution price. | Include in backtests and paper/live comparisons. | Slippage rises with volatility, size, and poor liquidity. |
| Trading costs | Brokerage, exchange fees, statutory charges, spread, slippage, financing, borrow, and data costs where relevant. | Calculate round-trip net P/L. | Check current fee schedules and jurisdiction-specific taxes. |
| Position sizing | Quantity chosen to keep exposure within a defined risk budget. | Simplified shares = `risk budget / (entry − stop)` for a long position, before costs and gap risk. | A stop price does not guarantee a loss cap; gap and liquidity risk remain. |
| Risk per trade | Planned loss under a defined adverse scenario. | Example: 1% of a ₹1,00,000 paper account = ₹1,000 planned risk. | A chosen percentage is not universally appropriate; real loss can exceed planned risk. |
| Stop-loss | Exit trigger or order intended to limit a position's loss. | Define trigger, order type, and gap assumptions. | Stop-market may slip; stop-limit may not execute. |
| Maximum drawdown | Largest peak-to-trough decline over a specified series. | `MDD = min(value / prior running peak − 1)`. | Path matters; recovery time and tail loss also matter. |
| Expected value | Probability-weighted payoff; for trades, use net outcomes and realistic probabilities. | EV per trade = `win probability × average win − loss probability × average loss − costs`, with mutually exhaustive outcomes. | Probabilities are estimated and may shift by regime. |
| Win rate / payoff ratio | Share of profitable trades / average win relative to average loss. | A high win rate can still lose if losses are much larger. | Always include costs, tail events, and sample uncertainty. |
| Operational risk | Failures in systems, data, orders, credentials, connectivity, or reconciliation. | Use logs, limits, duplicate-order protection, monitoring, and kill switches in simulations. | Technical reliability does not prove strategy profitability. |
| Trade journal | Record of hypothesis, context, sizing, execution, result, and adherence to rules. | Review batches of trades for process errors and outcome patterns. | Avoid cherry-picking or rewriting the original thesis afterward. |
| Emotional discipline | Process controls for fear, greed, FOMO, revenge trading, and overconfidence. | Set pre-committed limits and breaks; stop when rules require it. | Discipline cannot turn a negative-expectancy strategy into a positive one. |

## Stage 11 — Technical analysis and chart-reading workbook

**Core resources:** [NSE Technical Analysis course outline](https://www.nseindia.com/static/learn/certificate-program-in-technical-analysis), [NSE Technical Analysis and Chart Patterns](https://www.nseindia.com/static/learn/online-recorded-courses-technical-analysis-and-chart-patterns), [NISM Technical Analysis for Beginners](https://uatsystem.nism.ac.in/elp/technical-analysis-for-beginners.html), [StockCharts ChartSchool](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/), [Fidelity Technical Analysis](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/trading-investing/technical-analysis/using-technical-analysis). Related books: *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*, *Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques*, *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns*, *Evidence-Based Technical Analysis*.

### Read a chart in this order

1. **Instrument and data:** ticker, venue, timezone, session, adjustment policy, OHLCV source, and timestamp.
2. **Context:** broad index, sector, event calendar, liquidity, and volatility.
3. **Structure:** swing highs/lows, trend/range, gaps, and reaction zones.
4. **Pattern hypothesis:** identify the pattern using rules defined before the outcome.
5. **Confirmation and invalidation:** what exact close, level, or event would support or reject the hypothesis?
6. **Risk and execution:** entry assumption, exit logic, quantity, spread, slippage, costs, gap risk.
7. **Testing:** baseline, sample size, out-of-sample test, drawdown, robustness, and limitations.

### Candlestick anatomy

A candle shows **open, high, low, and close** over a selected time interval. The body spans open-to-close; the upper and lower shadows/wicks show extremes beyond the body.

- A bullish candle closes above its open; a bearish candle closes below its open. The color convention is configurable, so always check the chart legend.
- A long wick means price traded away from the body during the interval; it does not by itself prove rejection or reversal.
- A doji has open and close near each other under a chosen tolerance; it can indicate indecision but depends on context.
- Hammer, shooting star, engulfing, harami, morning/evening star and related names describe geometric formations. Their meaning depends on preceding trend, location, gap/session conventions, and confirmation rule.

### Chart patterns and their research interpretation

| Pattern family | What the shape describes | What to test / where useful | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Support/resistance zones | Areas where price previously reacted or traded heavily. | Define zones using an objective swing/volume rule; record later touches and breaks. | Drawing exact levels after the event; levels can fail. |
| Trendline / channel | Approximate directional path through swing points or parallel boundaries. | Define pivot selection and minimum touches; test break/retest rules. | Subjective line placement and hindsight. |
| Double top / bottom | Two attempts near a similar level, often with an intervening reaction. | Define allowable level difference, trough/peak confirmation, and breakout timing. | Similar-looking shapes occur in ranges without meaningful reversal. |
| Head-and-shoulders / inverse | Three swing structures with a middle extreme and a neckline concept. | Predefine pivots, neckline, confirmation, and target logic. | Subjective pattern selection; false breakouts. |
| Triangle | Converging boundaries around price swings. | Define slope/touch requirements and whether breakout direction is known only after confirmation. | Can break either way; pattern alone has no directional certainty. |
| Rectangle / range | Repeated movement between upper/lower zones. | Test range boundaries, false breaks, and range-trading rules. | Regime change can create large losses for mean-reversion assumptions. |
| Flag / pennant | Brief consolidation after a strong move. | Define prior impulse, consolidation duration, volume and breakout rules. | Visual selection bias and trend exhaustion. |
| Wedge | Converging boundaries, often sloping. | Define swing-point rules and test break direction empirically. | Wedges can resolve in either direction; labels are inconsistent. |
| Gap | Open or trade discontinuity between prices. | Classify session/event context and measure post-gap distribution. | Gaps may fill, extend, or remain open; none is guaranteed. |
| Candlestick setup | One or more candle bodies/wicks forming a named shape. | Test with preceding trend, location, confirmation, costs, and sample size. | Candle-only rules can be weak and highly context-dependent. |
| Fibonacci levels | Ratios projected over a selected price swing. | Fix swing selection algorithm and test whether levels add value beyond baseline. | Subjective anchor selection and multiple-testing risk. |
| Market profile / volume profile | Distribution of traded activity across price levels under provider-specific methodology. | Study value area, point of control, and session definition. | Profile calculations differ; not all volume data is complete. |
| Heikin-Ashi / Renko / point-and-figure | Transformed chart representations that smooth or filter price movement. | Learn construction, then test on executable raw prices. | Synthetic values may not be tradable prices and can delay signals. |

### Technical indicators: formula, purpose, and limitation

| Indicator | Simplified description/formula | Use | Do not infer |
|---|---|---|---|
| SMA | Mean of last `n` closes. | Trend smoothing and baseline comparisons. | It is not a forecast; it lags and whipsaws. |
| EMA | Exponentially weighted average; recent prices receive higher weights. | Similar trend use with greater recent sensitivity. | Faster response can mean more false signals. |
| RSI | Momentum oscillator derived from average gains/losses over a lookback. | Compare momentum and test defined divergence/threshold rules. | “Overbought” does not automatically mean sell; strong trends can stay extreme. |
| MACD | Difference between fast and slow EMAs; signal line is an EMA of MACD. | Trend/momentum comparison under defined parameters. | Crossovers lag and can whipsaw. |
| ATR | Average of true range, which accounts for prior-close gaps. | Volatility sizing and range comparison. | ATR has no direction and cannot guarantee a stop distance. |
| Bollinger Bands | Moving average plus/minus a chosen multiple of rolling standard deviation. | Study volatility expansion/contraction and relative price location. | Band touch alone is not a reversal signal. |
| ADX/DMI | Directional-movement framework with trend-strength measures. | Separate trend strength from direction. | Strong trend readings do not say whether price will rise or fall. |
| Stochastic | Close's position relative to a recent high-low range, with smoothing. | Momentum/range studies. | Extreme readings can persist. |
| VWAP | Volume-weighted average price over a defined session/anchor. | Intraday reference when data and session are appropriate. | Depends on complete volume, venue, session, and anchor. |
| OBV / volume indicators | Accumulate or transform volume using price direction or other rules. | Study participation hypotheses. | Venue volume may be incomplete or fragmented; indicators do not identify intent. |
| Realized volatility | Historical variability of returns over a defined window. | Compare current volatility with history and size risk. | Past volatility may not describe future tails. |
| Implied volatility | Volatility input that makes a pricing model align with observed option price. | Compare option prices across strikes/expiries and scenarios. | It is model-derived, not a direct guarantee of future realized volatility. |

### What to record for every chart screenshot or analysis

- Symbol, venue, chart provider, data timestamp, timezone, timeframe, adjustment policy.
- Trend/range classification and objective evidence.
- Marked swing points/zones and the rule used to mark them.
- Pattern name, exact definition, confirmation, invalidation, and alternative interpretation.
- Indicator settings and their reason.
- Relevant earnings/macro/expiry event context.
- Hypothetical entry/exit, quantity, stop assumptions, target logic, spread and slippage.
- Comparable baseline, number of examples, failed cases, out-of-sample result, and uncertainty.

### Technical-analysis testing checklist

- [ ] Define the signal before inspecting the test outcome.
- [ ] Use clean, correctly timestamped data.
- [ ] Handle splits, dividends, delisted instruments, and missing bars correctly.
- [ ] Avoid look-ahead bias and survivorship bias.
- [ ] Include spread, commissions, slippage, financing, and applicable taxes.
- [ ] Separate design, validation, and final out-of-sample periods chronologically.
- [ ] Compare with a simple baseline and report drawdown and turnover.
- [ ] Test sensitivity to parameters and market regimes.
- [ ] Document every variation tried, not only the best result.
- [ ] Treat a backtest as evidence about the past, not a promise about the future.

## Stage 12 — Index investing and index trading

**Resources:** [NSE indices](https://www.niftyindices.com/), [BSE indices](https://www.bseindia.com/), [S&P Dow Jones Indices](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/), [MSCI](https://www.msci.com/), [FTSE Russell](https://www.lseg.com/en/ftse-russell). Related books: *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing*, *Expected Returns*.

An **index** is a rule-based measure of a defined market or segment. Learn its universe, inclusion rules, weighting, rebalancing, corporate-action treatment, and whether it is a price-return or total-return index. A fund tracking an index is different from trading a futures/options contract based on that index.

- **Market-cap weighting:** larger market values have larger weights.
- **Free-float weighting:** weights reflect the provider's eligible public-float definition.
- **Equal weighting:** each constituent receives a similar target weight before drift.
- **Price weighting:** share price influences weights under the methodology.
- **Index futures basis:** futures price minus spot/index value, with interpretation depending on rates, dividends, time to expiry, and contract terms.
- **Index options:** contracts whose payoff depends on an index level; they do not represent direct ownership of all index constituents.
- **Practice:** read official methodology, reconstruct a sample index weight, compare price vs total returns, and calculate drawdown and tracking difference.

## Stage 13 — Derivatives, futures, and contract mechanics

**Core resources:** [CME Group Education](https://www.cmegroup.com/education.html), [CFTC Learn & Protect](https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect), [NSE derivatives](https://www.nseindia.com/), [SEBI investor education](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/iematerial.html). Related books: *Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives*, *Trading and Exchanges*.

| Term | Definition / use | Example or calculation | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Derivative | Contract whose value depends on an underlying asset, rate, index, or event. | Futures/options are common derivatives. | Leverage, complexity, and counterparty/market risk. |
| Forward | Customized bilateral future transaction. | Agree today on price/quantity/date for later exchange. | Counterparty and liquidity risk; terms are contract-specific. |
| Futures | Standardized exchange-traded contract with clearing and margin rules. | P/L often relates to price change × contract multiplier × quantity. | Daily settlement/margin can force cash needs before final thesis resolves. |
| Options | Contract giving buyer a right under terms and seller a corresponding obligation. | Call/put payoff depends on underlying, strike, premium, and expiry. | Buyers can lose premium; some short options can have very large losses. |
| Swap | Contract exchanging cash flows under a defined formula. | Interest-rate swap may exchange fixed and floating payments. | Counterparty, collateral, basis, and valuation risks. |
| Contract specifications | Official multiplier, tick, expiry, settlement, hours, and other rules. | Read the current exchange contract page before calculating P/L. | Contract details can change; never assume old lot size remains current. |
| Lot size / multiplier | Quantity represented by one contract. | P/L = points moved × multiplier × number of contracts, adjusted for contract convention. | A small price move can create large notional exposure. |
| Expiry | Date/time when contract terms reach expiry. | Confirm last trading day, exercise and settlement rules. | Liquidity and Greeks may change sharply near expiry. |
| Tick size | Minimum permitted price increment. | A 0.05 tick means quotes/trades move in permitted increments of 0.05, if that is the contract's current rule. | Verify current tick for the specific instrument. |
| Margin | Collateral required to support a position. | Margin is not the same as maximum possible loss or notional value. | Losses may exceed posted margin; margin can be raised. |
| Initial/maintenance margin | Opening collateral / minimum equity or collateral level under rules. | Falling below maintenance can trigger a margin call/liquidation. | Broker/exchange rules and stress add-ons vary. |
| Mark-to-market | Revaluation and settlement of gains/losses as required by contract rules. | Daily futures P/L may be credited/debited. | Cash-flow timing can be as important as final economics. |
| Cash vs physical settlement | Contract settles in cash or delivery of underlying/eligible instrument. | Read exact expiry procedure. | Delivery obligations and penalties can apply; never assume cash settlement. |
| Basis | Difference between related prices, often futures minus spot. | Track basis through time and expiry. | Basis can widen unexpectedly and may not converge smoothly. |
| Contango/backwardation | Futures curve above/below spot or a defined nearby contract, depending on convention. | Plot contracts by expiry and state the reference convention. | Curve shape reflects carry, supply/demand, and risk premia; not a simple forecast. |
| Hedging | Position intended to reduce a defined existing risk. | A producer may sell futures to reduce future selling-price uncertainty. | Hedge can be imperfect due to basis, size, timing, and correlation. |
| Speculation | Taking risk to benefit from a forecast or risk premium. | A directional futures position is speculative if not offsetting an existing exposure. | Leverage amplifies losses. |
| Arbitrage | Attempt to exploit price inconsistency across related instruments. | Compare cash-and-carry assumptions after financing, costs, borrow, and settlement. | Apparent arbitrage may reflect frictions, timing, or execution constraints. |
| Counterparty risk | Risk the other party fails to meet obligations. | Important for OTC and custodial arrangements. | Clearing reduces some risks but does not remove all systemic or operational risks. |
| Leverage | Exposure relative to capital/collateral. | Notional exposure ÷ capital. | Small adverse moves can cause large equity losses or liquidation. |
| Gap risk / margin call | Price jumps past expected levels / additional collateral is demanded. | Stress-test overnight moves and margin increases. | Stops may not execute at expected prices; forced liquidation can crystallize losses. |

## Stage 14 — Options from first principles

**Core resources:** [Cboe Options Institute](https://www.cboe.com/optionsinstitute/), [Options Industry Council — Options Basics](https://www.optionseducation.org/), [Options Industry Council — Greeks](https://prd-web.optionseducation.org/advancedconcepts/understanding-options-greeks), [NSE technical-analysis/derivatives education](https://www.nseindia.com/learn), [SEBI derivatives education](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/iematerial.html). Related books: *Option Volatility and Pricing*, *Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives*, *Dynamic Hedging*.

### Contract terms

| Term | Meaning / calculation | Example and use | Risk / nuance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Call / put | Call gives the buyer the right to buy under terms; put gives the buyer the right to sell under terms. | Used for directional exposure or hedging. | Buyer rights and seller obligations depend on contract style. |
| Buyer / seller | Buyer pays premium for rights; seller receives premium and accepts obligations. | Draw both sides' payoffs before studying a strategy. | Premium received is not automatically profit; losses can exceed it. |
| Strike / expiry | Contract price and expiration date/time. | Payoff is evaluated relative to strike and underlying at expiry. | Confirm current exchange contract specifications. |
| Premium | Option price. | Option buyer's maximum loss is generally the premium paid for a long, unhedged option, plus costs. | Seller's risk depends on structure and may be very large. |
| Intrinsic / time value | Call intrinsic `max(S−K,0)`; put intrinsic `max(K−S,0)`; time value is premium minus intrinsic value under common convention. | If S=110, K=100, call premium=13, intrinsic=10, time value=3. | Market quotes, exercise style, dividends, rates, and conventions matter. |
| Moneyness | ITM/ATM/OTM relationship between underlying and strike. | Call ITM if S>K; put ITM if S<K, with ATM conventions varying. | Moneyness does not determine whether the option is cheap or expensive. |
| Payoff / profit | Payoff is value at expiry before premium; profit includes premium and costs. | Long call expiry profit = `max(S_T−K,0) − premium` per share, before costs. | Keep multiplier/lot size in calculations. |
| Breakeven | Expiry underlying price where payoff offsets premium/costs. | Long call breakeven at expiry is approximately `K + premium`. | Not the probability of profit or a guaranteed exit level. |
| European / American | Exercise only at expiry / exercise may be allowed earlier under contract terms. | Affects valuation and exercise behavior. | Use actual contract style; do not assume all listed options are alike. |
| Exercise / assignment | Exercise invokes rights; assignment allocates obligations to a short position under contract rules. | Understand broker/exchange process before expiry. | Early exercise and assignment risks vary. |
| Covered call | Long underlying plus short call. | Premium income in exchange for capped upside over strike, with downside on underlying. | Not a risk-free income strategy. |
| Protective put | Long underlying plus long put. | Put can limit downside below strike, subject to premium and contract terms. | Protection costs money and may be imperfect. |
| Spread | Combination of options with different strikes/expiries or sides. | Vertical spread can cap both upside and downside under defined terms. | Maximum loss/profit must be calculated for the exact contract and costs. |
| Straddle / strangle | Long or short call+put structures at same/different strikes. | Often studied for volatility exposure. | Direction, implied volatility, timing, and costs all matter. |
| Butterfly / condor | Multi-leg structures with defined strike relationships. | Study payoff across a range of expiry prices. | Execution across legs and liquidity can alter realized outcome. |
| Calendar / diagonal | Options with different expiries and sometimes strikes. | Study term structure and time decay. | Expiry and volatility interactions make simple payoff intuition incomplete. |
| Ratio spread / collar | Unequal option quantities / stock plus options to shape payoff. | Calculate every leg's payoff and margin. | Ratio structures can have large or asymmetric losses. |
| Put-call parity | Relationship between call, put, underlying, strike, rates, dividends, and expiry for comparable contracts. | Used to check pricing consistency under assumptions. | Exercise style, dividends, funding, and transaction costs matter. |
| Arbitrage bounds | No-arbitrage constraints on option prices under stated assumptions. | Compare market price with model-consistent bounds. | Apparent violations may reflect stale data, spreads, or nonmatching contracts. |
| Early exercise | Exercising an American-style option before expiry. | Analyze dividends, rates, and intrinsic/time value. | Contract-specific; don't exercise based only on ITM status. |

### Payoff formulas (per underlying unit, before fees)

- Long call: `max(S_T − K, 0) − premium`.
- Long put: `max(K − S_T, 0) − premium`.
- Short call: `premium − max(S_T − K, 0)`.
- Short put: `premium − max(K − S_T, 0)`.

Here `S_T` is underlying price at expiry and `K` is strike. Multiply by contract multiplier and quantity, then subtract transaction costs and account for settlement/tax treatment.

**Illustrative example:** a call has strike ₹100 and premium ₹8. At expiry with underlying ₹120, payoff is ₹20 and profit before costs is ₹12 per unit. At expiry with underlying ₹105, payoff is ₹5 and profit is −₹3. At expiry with underlying ₹95, payoff is zero and loss is the ₹8 premium. This ignores fees, lot multiplier, early exercise, and settlement details.

## Stage 15 — Options chain and Greeks

| Field / Greek | Meaning | How to use | What not to infer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strike rows / call-put columns | Listed contracts organized by strike and expiry. | Compare matching expiries/strikes and record timestamp. | A chain snapshot is not a complete picture of positions. |
| Bid / ask / LTP | Current bid, current ask, and last traded price. | Use bid/ask to understand potential execution; check quote size and freshness. | LTP can be stale and may not be executable. |
| Volume | Contracts traded over the reporting interval. | Study activity with liquidity and quote context. | Volume alone does not reveal direction or trader identity. |
| Open interest (OI) | Outstanding contracts under exchange reporting rules. | Track changes over time with price, volume, and contract details. | OI alone does not say whether the market is bullish/bearish or reveal “operator” intent. |
| Change in OI | Difference from a stated prior OI observation. | Confirm reporting timestamps and contract/expiry matching. | New OI does not identify who initiated the trade or motive. |
| Implied volatility | Model input inferred from option price. | Compare strikes and expiries; study skew and term structure. | IV is not a guaranteed forecast of realized volatility. |
| Historical/realized volatility | Variation measured from historical underlying returns. | Compare with IV under consistent windows and annualization. | Past volatility may not persist. |
| Delta | Local option-price sensitivity to underlying price under model assumptions. | Estimate small-move exposure and hedge ratios. | Delta is not a guaranteed probability of expiring ITM. |
| Gamma | Change in delta for a change in underlying. | Understand how hedge ratios change as price moves. | Gamma can be highly nonlinear near expiry/strike. |
| Theta | Sensitivity to time passage holding other inputs fixed. | Study time decay in a model scenario. | Real markets change price, volatility, and rates simultaneously. |
| Vega | Sensitivity to a change in implied volatility. | Stress-test IV increases/decreases. | Vega is model-based and depends on strike/expiry and units. |
| Rho | Sensitivity to interest rates under the pricing model. | More relevant in some longer-dated contracts. | Rates are not the only input changing option prices. |
| Skew / smile | Variation of implied volatility across strikes. | Compare relative option pricing across moneyness. | Shape is market-specific and changes over time. |
| Term structure | Variation of IV across expiries. | Compare near/far expiries and event risk. | A snapshot can be stale and not a certain volatility forecast. |
| Black–Scholes / binomial | Option-pricing models with different assumptions and numerical structure. | Learn assumptions, price theoretical scenarios, infer IV. | Model price is not the same as executable market price. |
| Expiry/settlement | Contract's final trading, exercise, and settlement process. | Check current official contract specification. | Last-day liquidity, assignment, and margin risk can change sharply. |

### How to analyze an options chain without overclaiming

1. Record underlying, expiry, timestamp, strike, bid/ask, LTP, volume, OI, OI change, IV, and Greeks where available.
2. Remove stale/illiquid quotes from any quantitative study under a predeclared rule.
3. Compare like-for-like expiries and moneyness; do not compare unlike contracts without adjustment.
4. Calculate payoff and theoretical sensitivities using explicit assumptions.
5. Inspect liquidity, spread, contract multiplier, margin, and settlement rules.
6. Treat put-call ratio, max pain, OI “walls,” and volume spikes as descriptive hypotheses—not guaranteed support/resistance or proof of market intent.
7. Test whether any proposed signal has predictive value out of sample after costs.

## Stage 16 — Advanced volatility, hedging, and risk

**Resources:** [Cboe Options Institute](https://www.cboe.com/optionsinstitute/), [Options Industry Council](https://www.optionseducation.org/), [CME Education](https://www.cmegroup.com/education.html), [QuantLib](https://www.quantlib.org/). Related books: *Volatility Trading*, *Dynamic Hedging*, *Paul Wilmott Introduces Quantitative Finance*.

- **Volatility trading:** taking exposure to changes in implied/realized volatility or their relationship; direction and volatility are distinct.
- **Delta hedging:** adjusting underlying exposure to offset model-estimated delta. It must be rebalanced as delta changes and can incur transaction costs.
- **Gamma exposure:** describes sensitivity of delta to underlying moves; large gamma can make hedging needs change rapidly.
- **Vega exposure:** sensitivity to implied-volatility changes across the option portfolio.
- **Theta:** model sensitivity to time passage, holding other inputs fixed; not a guaranteed daily income stream.
- **Volatility risk premium:** observed tendency in some markets for implied volatility to exceed subsequent realized volatility over selected samples; it is not guaranteed and can be overwhelmed by tail losses.
- **Term structure / skew:** IV variation by expiry and strike. Investigate event dates, supply/demand, crash protection, and contract details.
- **Dispersion:** strategies comparing index volatility with component volatility/correlation; requires precise contract, correlation, and execution assumptions.
- **Dynamic hedging:** repeated rebalancing; evaluate hedge error, transaction costs, discrete jumps, and liquidity.
- **Stress testing:** shock underlying, volatility, rates, correlations, spreads, and margin simultaneously.
- **Tail risk:** rare severe outcomes; use historical crises, scenario shocks, and limitations of fitted distributions.
- **Model risk:** risk that assumptions, calibration, implementation, or data make a model misleading.

**Practice:** simulate a small hypothetical option portfolio under changes in underlying price, IV, time, rates, and liquidity. Report base case, adverse scenarios, hedge changes, estimated costs, and model limitations. Never interpret a theoretical Greek as an exact live P/L guarantee.

## Stage 17 — IPO, fund, and market research: timing and source hierarchy

When facts are time-sensitive, use this hierarchy:

1. **Official issuer filing / exchange disclosure / regulator circular** for legally relevant facts.
2. **Official exchange/clearing/market-data documentation** for contract specifications, settlement, and index methodology.
3. **Audited annual report and notes** for historical financial data.
4. **Official fund factsheet/prospectus** for fees, holdings, benchmark, risk, and mandate.
5. **Central-bank/statistical-agency release** for macro data.
6. **Academic paper or established textbook** for theory and empirical context.
7. **Secondary analysis** for interpretation, clearly distinguished from primary evidence.
8. **Social media, forums, and influencer commentary** only as leads to investigate—not as a substitute for evidence.

Always record publication date, data period, retrieval date, and the difference between what the source states and what you infer.

## Stage 18 — Autonomous and algorithmic trading: safe learning sequence

**Resources:** [NSE algo-trading procedures](https://www.nse.in/static/trade/platform-services-non-neat-decision-support-tools-algorithm-trading), [QuantConnect LEAN](https://github.com/QuantConnect/Lean), [Backtrader](https://www.backtrader.com/), [vectorbt](https://vectorbt.dev/), [Freqtrade](https://www.freqtrade.io/), [Hummingbot](https://hummingbot.org/), [CCXT](https://github.com/ccxt/ccxt).

1. **Manual research:** formulate a clear hypothesis with no order automation.
2. **Historical analysis:** clean data, define signals, compare a baseline, and report bias/costs.
3. **Backtest:** simulate orders and fills with realistic timing and fees.
4. **Paper trading:** use a sandbox/simulator and record every simulated order.
5. **Operational tests:** handle disconnects, duplicate orders, partial fills, stale data, clock errors, and rejected orders.
6. **Risk controls:** set position/notional limits, loss limits, kill switch, audit logs, and alerting.
7. **Compliance review:** check current exchange, broker, regulator, API, data-license, and jurisdictional requirements.
8. **Only then consider whether live deployment is appropriate:** do not treat it as a default next step.

### What “autonomous” should mean in a controlled system

An automated system may ingest data, generate signals, propose/submit orders, monitor positions, and log actions. It should not operate without explicit limits, monitoring, secure credentials, reconciliation, and a way to stop. A backtest or demo account does not prove live profitability or compliance.

**Minimum simulator controls:** no real-money credentials; order-size caps; duplicate-order prevention; maximum open positions; daily stop condition; stale-data detection; logging; restart recovery; and a manual kill switch. Keep API secrets out of source control and do not give withdrawal permissions to a trading key.

- Check current tax treatment by asset, holding period, turnover, jurisdiction, and personal status with official guidance or a qualified tax professional.
